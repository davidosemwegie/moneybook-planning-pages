8. Before you buy — spoken script

A spoken draft about wanted purchases, money still to come, and payments that stack up.

Spoken adaptation · First draft. Based on Chapter 8. This is developed prose, not a raw transcript.

The script

You want the thing. The headphones, the camera, the car. Okay. What happens to the rest of your money if you buy it?

I love cars. They make me happy. I don't think you have to pretend a purchase is going to earn money before you're allowed to want it. Your quality of life matters too.

But I don't want you to buy something you wanted and then hate the payments. So let's think about it now.

For an unplanned purchase over two hundred and fifty dollars, I try to have enough to pay for it twice. If the headphones cost three hundred and fifty, I want seven hundred available.

That's my personal rule. I don't get it right every time. Something goes on sale, and suddenly I'm explaining to myself why I need it. So the rule gives me a pause.

I'm talking about an unplanned purchase. If you've been saving for something, check that plan and what the purchase leaves behind. I'm not saying you need to save for the same thing twice. An urgent need is a different decision too. My rule isn't a reason to leave an essential need unmet.

And we need to check what “available” means. If you've got seven hundred in the account and the rent needs seven hundred, you don't have seven hundred for headphones.

Here's an invented example from the chapter. You have eighteen hundred in cash. Eleven hundred is already assigned to bills, debt, tax, saving, giving, and other commitments. That leaves seven hundred free.

Buy the three-hundred-and-fifty-dollar headphones, and you have three hundred and fifty free afterward. The account still has fourteen hundred and fifty in it, but eleven hundred of that still has a job. Don't spend it twice.

You could buy a suitable two-hundred-and-fifty-dollar pair and keep four hundred and fifty free. You could keep what you have and leave all seven hundred alone. Those are choices. Write down which one you're making and why.

Now, let's talk about money that hasn't come in yet.

I've fallen into this doing software work on the side. You have a conversation about a job. You expect a payment. In your mind, you've already started spending it. Then the money doesn't arrive when you expected. The expense is still there.

Let's say you're expecting ten thousand from a job and you spend two thousand on a computer before you're paid. Those numbers illustrate the pattern. The money still has to arrive, while the purchase has already happened.

If the purchase can wait, wait for the payment. Then count the costs, tax, and other commitments it still has to cover. Next week's paycheck isn't money in the account today either.

And don't use a card limit as the answer. A two-thousand-dollar limit doesn't tell you whether a fifteen-hundred-dollar camera fits your own plan. Go back to the money you can use and how you'll pay the bill.

Buy now, pay later needs the same attention. It's borrowing. One small payment might seem fine. Then you get another, and another. They stack up.

Write every payment down. The amount today. The later amounts and dates. The full amount you owe. The terms and any costs. Then put those dates beside the payments you've already agreed to make.

The chapter invents a plan for the three-hundred-and-fifty-dollar headphones: four payments of eighty-seven fifty, two weeks apart, with no interest or fees assumed. It's a maths example, not a product offer.

After the first payment, you still owe two hundred and sixty-two fifty for the headphones. If another sixty-dollar payment and a ninety-dollar payment fall before the next instalment, those three payments need two hundred and thirty-seven fifty altogether. And your other bills are still there.

Check the actual agreement. Fees and automatic-payment arrangements differ. A small instalment doesn't tell you the full cost.

If you're already struggling with required payments, I'd go back to that problem before taking on another loan for something optional.

The same thinking applies to a car. Yes, it can be something you enjoy. But count the whole cost and the whole loan. Insurance, fuel, maintenance, the things you'll have to pay to keep using it. Then look at what would happen in a lower-income month.

Would you still enjoy the car if its payments took away something else you care about?

Okay, now pick one purchase and use workbook page eighteen, or plain paper. Name it and say why you want it. Work out the money available after commitments. Compare the choices and their full costs. Put any payments on the calendar.

Then make a decision: buy, save first, choose something else, wait, or check a missing detail. Give yourself a next action and a date.

You can want the thing. I just want you to see what getting it would mean for the rest of your life.
