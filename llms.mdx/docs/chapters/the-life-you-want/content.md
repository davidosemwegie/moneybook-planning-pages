# 2. The life you want (/docs/chapters/the-life-you-want)



*First draft. Based on [Note 3](/docs/raw-thoughts/note-3) and [Note 4](/docs/raw-thoughts/note-4). Sources and example assumptions are at the end.*

Okay, so you've put your money on paper. You can see what you have, what comes in, and what you owe. Now where are you trying to go?

Sometimes I look at a decision and wonder, what was the plan? What were you trying to get to? It baffles me how you can put so much effort into something without knowing what you want it to give you. That's why I think we need to spend some time on this question before we start talking about earning more money.

You might want to travel, get home in time to eat with your family, or look at your bank account without worrying about whether the bills are covered. All of those are things we can work towards, but “I want more money” doesn't tell us which one matters to you. We need to get specific enough to picture the life, estimate what it costs, and choose something to work on next.

## Start with an ordinary day [#start-with-an-ordinary-day]

Let's work backwards from where you'd love to be five or ten years from now, or a nearer date if that fits what you're working on. I mean getting into the details of an ordinary day: where you live, what time you wake up, and what happens once you're out of bed.

Let's say you want to get up at 5:30, go to the gym, and come home while your partner is getting the kids ready for school. You drop them off on the way to work, do your job, and get home in time to have dinner with your family every day. That might be exactly what you want. Now we have something to work with, because we can start asking what it would take to have that day.

Some of it will cost money, and some of it will need your time. Where would you have to live? How far could you travel to work and still make it home? Write about those things along with the people you want to spend time with and what you want your evenings to look like.

Of course, you might not want kids, or you might love the job you already have. You may want more time to look after someone. That's fine. Write the life you want, including the responsibilities you want to keep, so we're working towards something that matters to you.

## Be honest about what matters to you [#be-honest-about-what-matters-to-you]

Maybe you care about cars, or you want to buy nice clothes. Maybe you don't care about any of that and just want to travel. People want different things, and I don't think we should be shaming someone because their idea of a good life is different from ours. You don't have to become a millionaire to have a goal worth working towards.

But give me the whole picture. If being there for your kids matters to you, put that beside the car you want to drive. Otherwise, we could choose an income goal, find work that pays it, and discover that the hours keep you away from the very thing you wanted the money for.

Once you've written it down, decide what matters most and what you could change. The school pickup might matter more than the car, or you might still want the car but be fine waiting longer for it. Knowing that gives you some room to make decisions when we work out the cost.

## Put a cost beside the life [#put-a-cost-beside-the-life]

Now we can put some amounts beside that life. First decide whether you're planning for your own money or for the household, and use the same choice throughout. If someone else will cover part of a shared cost, write down how much and whether you've agreed on it. We need to know whose money pays for which part.

Start with costs you already know from your bills and account records. For something you don't pay for yet, find a current price and check what it includes. You're allowed to estimate; just write “estimate” beside the amount. If you don't know, put “not sure yet” and come back to it. Leaving a cost out would make the life look cheaper without making it any cheaper to live.

Include home costs, food, transport, care, health, giving, and the things you enjoy, along with payments you still expect to owe. If part of your goal is to have money saved, that also needs a place in the monthly plan. We'll include less frequent costs too, so the bills that arrive once a year don't disappear from the calculation.

### Give every amount a time period [#give-every-amount-a-time-period]

A monthly bill can go straight in the monthly column. If a recurring cost is $2,400 a year, divide it by 12 and put its $200 monthly share there. Keep the actual due date beside it, because spreading the amount across a calculation doesn't change when someone expects you to pay.

One-time costs need their own list. If the life you described means moving, for example, you may need money up front as well as the new monthly rent. Write down the cost, what you've already set aside for it, and how many months you have left to save the rest.

Suppose the goal costs $6,000 and you've already saved $1,200. That leaves $4,800, which comes to $200 a month over 24 months, without assuming any interest. Put that $200 inside your monthly saving total once. Now you can see how the one-time goal affects the amount you need each month.

### Use money you can plan with [#use-money-you-can-plan-with]

When you compare the cost of that life with what you earn, use money you can plan with. If an employer pays you, that's your take-home pay after taxes and other payroll deductions. Gross pay is the amount before those deductions, so using it here would leave you planning to spend money that doesn't all reach you.

If costs or tax still need to come out, use the notes you made in Chapter 1 to account for them first. If you don't know the tax amount, keep the comparison marked as incomplete until you check it. There's no single percentage we can guess for everybody, and this exercise won't calculate how much salary or business sales would produce your take-home target.

As you do that, check you're only counting each cost once. A car payment listed under transport shouldn't appear again under debt payments, and health insurance already deducted from your pay shouldn't be subtracted again. The total balance you owe also stays separate from the amount you plan to pay this month.

## Let's walk through Michelle's plan [#lets-walk-through-michelles-plan]

Let's say Michelle works in customer service and takes home $3,000 a month. She wants to spend her working day thinking, researching, and writing, and she wants to take her kids to school and be available when they need her. If the school calls, being able to show up matters to her. She doesn't want her work to get in the way of that.

She'd also like to drive a Range Rover, have some help cleaning the house, and save money each month. She doesn't care as much about travel, so that gets less of her budget. Those are her choices; your list may look completely different.

Michelle and the amounts in this example are fictional. We'll assume her partner has agreed to contribute $2,400 a month to the household plan and that the money is available. If you're making your own shared plan, check that contribution together before building the rest around it.

**All amounts below are made-up planning assumptions in generic dollars, not local price quotes.**

| Part of Michelle's chosen life | Monthly amount | What this example includes                                                                 |
| ------------------------------ | -------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Home                           |         $2,200 | Rent, utilities, phone, and internet                                                       |
| Food and household supplies    |           $600 | Groceries and everyday supplies                                                            |
| Transport                      |         $1,100 | An assumed $800 car payment plus $300 for fuel, insurance, and maintenance                 |
| Health and care                |           $400 | Costs not already deducted from pay, including planned care for the children               |
| Cleaning help                  |           $800 | A chosen monthly allowance; no weekly price or frequency is implied                        |
| Gym, clothes, and hobbies      |           $200 | Chosen personal spending                                                                   |
| Giving and gifts               |           $100 | This household's chosen amount                                                             |
| Less frequent costs            |           $200 | $2,400 a year for items not counted above, such as school costs and a small trip           |
| Other debt payments            |           $300 | Existing payments, excluding the car payment already listed                                |
| Saving                         |         $2,500 | $200 for the moving goal below and $2,300 for other saving goals, including a cash reserve |
| **Total monthly target**       |     **$8,400** | **The household amount needed for these assumptions**                                      |

Her moving goal uses the $6,000 example we just worked through. With $1,200 set aside and 24 months to save the remaining $4,800, she needs $200 a month. We've already included that in the $2,500 saving row, so don't add it on top of the $8,400.

We're putting a cost on a future version of Michelle's life here; her spending today may look very different. The table assumes no further up-front purchase costs, which she'd need to check against an actual car quote or move. She'd also need to check each allowance before committing to it. For now, these amounts let us work through the decision.

## Now we can see the gap [#now-we-can-see-the-gap]

With a household target of **$8,400 a month** and her partner's assumed contribution of **$2,400**, Michelle needs to bring in **$6,000 a month** after deductions and any costs or tax still to cover. Her current take-home pay is **$3,000**, so the difference she needs to work on is **$3,000 a month**.

Okay, now she has something to work with. She can look at ways to earn more, change part of the life she's priced, or give herself longer to get there. She may also check one of those estimates and find that it was wrong. That's a reason to work through the number again, not to throw away the whole plan.

And remember why she started. If she finds a way to bring in $6,000 but can never do the school pickup, she still has a decision to make. She wanted to be available for her children. We can't forget that just because the income reaches the number on the page.

The $2,500 saving choice belongs to Michelle's example; it isn't an amount everyone needs to save. Even if she reaches her monthly income target, she still has to set the money aside over time before her moving fund is ready.

## Your turn [#your-turn]

Now try that with the life you described. Workbook pages [5: My chosen life](/docs/workbook/my-chosen-life), [6: What that life costs](/docs/workbook/what-that-life-costs), and [7: My target and next step](/docs/workbook/my-target-and-next-step) give you space to work through it. If you're using plain paper, use the same page names and follow these steps.

1. **Describe the day.** Start with your name or household, today's date, and the date you're working towards. Then write about your morning, your work or other responsibilities, your evening, and your time off. Give yourself enough detail to see what you want your days to look like. As you read it back, mark what matters most and what you'd be willing to change, because those choices will help when you see the cost.
2. **List the costs.** Beside each part of the life you want, write an amount, how often you'd pay it, its monthly share, and where the estimate came from. Use one currency throughout so you can add the figures. Include saving and payments you expect to owe, and note who would cover any shared costs. If you don't know an amount yet, leave that gap visible rather than letting it disappear from the plan.
3. **List the one-time goals.** For each goal, write the full cost, the money already set aside for it, what you still need, and how many months are left. If you've already set aside enough, you have zero left to find. Otherwise, divide what you still need by the months left and include that monthly amount in saving once. Check which money you've assigned to each goal; the same savings can't fund two things at once. If you don't know the cost or timing, mark the result incomplete. And if the goal is due now, write down the immediate shortfall. There are no months left to spread it across, so dividing by zero won't help you plan for it.
4. **Compare like with like.** Add your monthly costs, payments, and saving to get the target. Beside it, put income and support for the same person or household and the same period, allowing for known costs and tax still to cover. If your income is uneven, use a range from your records so you're looking at more than your best month. Now subtract that income from the target. A positive result shows the gap; a negative result means the listed income is more than the estimated target. Keep any missing costs and payment dates beside the comparison, because that result doesn't settle them.
5. **Choose one next step.** Look over what you've written and choose one number to check or one change to explore. Give that action a date, then set a date to review the whole page too. Prices and your circumstances can change, so you'll need to come back to the estimate as you learn more. We aren't fixing the next ten years of your life on this sheet.

You can stop with a partial plan if you've written down what you still need to find out. You don't need to invent a number just to fill the page. Keep the gaps visible so you know what to come back and check.

## Where to go next [#where-to-go-next]

If you have no current income or support, finding income is the next priority. Keep the life you described, but don't wait until every future estimate is finished before taking that step. You have things to pay for now too.
