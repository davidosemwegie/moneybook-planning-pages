3. Find and grow your income

Find a source for now, compare your options, and choose one next step.

First draft · Chapter 3.

If you have no income coming in, the first thing we need to figure out is how to get some. It's hard to make plans for the future when you have no way to cover what's happening right now. Keep the life you described in Chapter 2, but let's start with what could bring money in from where you are today.

If money already comes in, we have a different question to work through. You might need more money, different hours, or work you enjoy more. Look at the gap you wrote down and the day you want to have. That will help us decide which change to work on, instead of chasing a bigger number without asking what comes with it.

You don't need to settle the rest of your career in this chapter. I want you to leave with one step you can take and a reason for taking it.

If no money comes in

I think that if you can work, need income, and have nothing coming in, you need to apply for work you can do. Some people aren't going to like that, especially if they've spent years getting a degree and had a particular company or role in mind. But if you're passing over suitable jobs simply because the company isn't the one you pictured, I'm sorry, that doesn't make sense to me. Apply. Give yourself a chance to earn something while you work towards the next thing.

Now, being a stay-at-home parent is a full-time responsibility. If that's what you want and it works for your household, there's nothing wrong with it. Studying can also take all the time you have. Your health, care duties, transport, or permission to work may limit what's possible too. A job you can't get to or fit around care doesn't become an option just because someone tells you to take it.

I'm talking about the work you can do. Write down the hours you have, where you can get to, and any costs you'd have to cover before the first payment arrives. Then look at the roles and employers that fit those limits. You may need help with an application or support while you search, and those are steps you can take as well.

Even a temporary job can be part of the plan. Taking it doesn't mean you've decided to stay there forever or given up on the life you want. You can earn from something else and keep looking for the role you want, after checking that the hours, costs, and terms of the temporary work make sense for you.

If there's already a gap for food, housing, or other essentials, put that on the page now. A possible job next month won't pay this week's bill, so check the help available where you live while you work on getting income. We need to deal with what's happening now as well as where you're trying to go.

What are you actually trying to get to?

I had a conversation with someone at Starbucks about having a goal and choosing how to get there. Sometimes you get so set on one way of doing something that you don't stop to consider another route. You might not even know another route exists.

Think about trying to reach the top of a sand dune in a Ferrari. You've decided you want the Ferrari, and now you're putting all your effort into getting it up the sand. But what was the goal? If it was getting to the top, why are you so attached to doing it in that car? You could at least consider a Jeep or an ATV before spending all that time trying to make the Ferrari work.

For me, it was being a tech founder. I'd put so much effort into that idea, but when I thought about what mattered to me, it was having a say in how a product works, how it's designed, and how it's built. Being a founder was the way I'd pictured getting that decision-making power. Once I understood what I wanted from it, I could ask whether there were other ways to get there.

So when you look at an income option, ask what you want it to give you. The pay matters, but so might the hours, the chance to make things, or being there when your children get home. If several routes could give you those things, consider them. You don't have to start a business because someone else did, or leave a job that works for your life just to prove you're ambitious.

If an employer pays you

You can start with the job you have as well as looking at other jobs. More hours, a higher rate, or a different role could help, but think about what each would mean for your week. If the extra hours take away something you need at home, include that in the decision.

Before a pay conversation, gather examples of the work you've done and check what comparable roles pay in your area. That gives you something specific to discuss. Compare the whole offer too, including benefits, hours, and travel, so you can see what you'd gain and what you'd have to change. There isn't always room to negotiate, but you can prepare to ask.

Here's a sample way to start:

I'd like to talk about my pay. Here are the responsibilities I've taken on and the results I can show. What would need to happen for my pay to increase, and when could we review it?

If another role needs a skill you don't yet have, read the job listings before paying for training. See what employers ask for and how much of it you already know. “I need to learn more” can go on forever; finding a specific skill you need lets you decide how to practice it and whether the training you're considering would help.

If you want to earn from your own work

Okay, so you want to write, edit, build an app, or sell a service. Who would pay you, and what would they get? Work backwards from that. If you're going to spend time making something, you need to figure out whether someone wants it enough to pay for it.

Write an answer a person could understand, then choose a way to test it with time and money you can afford to use. You might speak to possible customers, show them a sample, or offer one small paid job with a clear scope. You can learn from that before committing more of what you have.

As you do it, keep track of the money and the work involved. What customers pay you is revenue, and some of that may have to cover business costs. Tax may still need to come out of what's left. Until you've accounted for those amounts, you don't know how much of the payment you can use for your own plan.

Let's use an invented example with generic dollars. Suppose you complete four editing jobs at $150 each. All four customers pay during the period you're reviewing.

This batch of workAmount
Four paid jobs × $150$600 received
All business costs for this batch, assumed for the example−$120
Left before tax$480
Time delivering the work8 hours
Time finding customers and doing admin4 hours
Total time12 hours

Divide the $480 by all 12 hours and you get $40 before tax for each hour you counted. In this example, that's what remains after the business costs we've listed; it isn't a wage offer or take-home pay. If you discover more costs or hours, change the calculation. And you still need to find the next customer. Four people paying you this time doesn't tell you that four more will come next week.

That's the work I want you to think through: finding a customer, doing what you agreed to do, and checking what's left after the time and costs involved. Keep writing or building if it matters to you, but keep hoped-for earnings separate from money you have today. You can work towards an income goal without treating it as income you've already earned.

Income can come from more than one place

As you compare options, keep using the four labels from Chapter 1: Employer, Own work, Assets, and Support / retirement. Hourly pay and salary both sit under Employer, and you can use several labels if money comes from different places.

If you already receive money from an asset, start with what it paid you and record any costs or tax still to cover. We need that payment to know what it contributes, because owning something doesn't tell us that it pays you each month. Work with the receipts you have here; don't treat this exercise as a reason to buy an investment to fill an immediate gap.

If you receive support or retirement payments, include the amounts you can use and check the provider's terms before assuming a payment will change or continue. If you're looking for US programs, USAGov's benefit finder links to possible benefits and application information. Elsewhere, check your local official services.

Help with a cost still matters, even when nobody pays cash into your account; just record that noncash help separately from money you can spend. Keep borrowed money and money taken from savings separate from ongoing income too. They change today's balance without creating a new source of earnings.

You can return to Chapter 1's income map if you're unsure where a payment belongs. These groups help you build the plan, while a tax office may classify the same payment differently.

When does the money arrive?

A monthly total can look fine while one week is difficult because the payment hasn't arrived yet. Write down the date as well as the amount, and mark each payment as received, expected, or uncertain. You may have done the work and sent the invoice, but until it's paid, you still don't have that money available.

If payments vary, look at several periods in your records so you can see the low periods alongside the better ones. Don't build the whole plan around your best month and assume every month will go that way. We'll work through payment dates in Chapter 4; for now, collect them and keep money still to come separate from money you already have.

Back to Michelle

Remember Michelle, our fictional reader from Chapter 2. The household life she priced costs $8,400 a month. Her partner's agreed contribution is $2,400, leaving $6,000 for her to cover. With $3,000 in take-home pay from customer service, she has a gap of $3,000 a month.

But Michelle also hates the work she's doing. She wants to spend her day writing and researching, and she wants to be available for school pickup. She could come home angry, spend the evening scrolling or watching TV, and do the same thing again tomorrow. I don't think that gets her where she wants to go. She needs to give some time to figuring out what else she could do, while remembering that her current job still brings money in.

Let's give her three routes to check against both the income goal and the day she wants. These are possibilities to investigate, not claims about available jobs or market pay.

Possible routeWhat she needs to find outA first step
A writing or research jobRequired skills, actual pay, hours, and school-pickup fitCompare three current job listings
Paid editing alongside her jobWhether someone will pay for a defined piece of work, and what it takes to deliverMake one sample and speak to three possible customers
Her own book or blogWho it serves and how it might eventually earn moneyTest one piece with its intended readers

In this example, she decides to explore editing while keeping her current job. She can give it two hours this week without spending any money on the first check. By Friday, she'll make a short sample using text she owns and ask three possible customers about their needs.

Then on Sunday, she can look at what happened. Did anyone have the problem she wants to solve, and was there interest in paying her for a defined job? Would doing that work fit her week? If the answers don't support it, she can change the offer or look more closely at the job route.

She still has the $3,000 gap, and we can't give her the fictional $480 from the editing example just because she's made a plan. What she's done is choose something she can try, with a point at which she'll review it. That gives her information for the next decision before she puts more time or money into it.

Your turn

Take what you've learned about your options and work through it on workbook page 8. My income options and page 9. My next income step. If you're using plain paper, use those page names and the headings below. We're going to narrow the options to something you can try.

  1. My starting point. Write your reader path and the sources you already have, then say whether you need money for now or are working towards a change for later. If you've done Chapter 2, bring over your target and gap so you can refer to them as you compare choices. If you haven't, you can still start here with the need you have today.
  2. My limits. Think through what it would take to fit this step into your life. Write down the hours you have, care needs, travel, work requirements, and how much money you can afford to put into it. If there's no money available, write “none.” That belongs in the plan because it affects which options you can try.
  3. Two options. For each option, write who would pay you, what you'd do, and the costs and time involved. Beside that, explain what evidence you have and what you still don't know, so you can see what needs checking. Keep expected earnings separate from your current income. If only one option is feasible, record why; you don't need to invent a second one to fill the page.
  4. One action. Choose something you can do by a date, such as finishing an application, having a pay or customer conversation, or checking with a support provider. Give it a time limit and a spending limit, so you've decided how much you can put into this step before you start.
  5. A review date. Decide what you'll check and when you'll come back to it. Keep payment dates on a separate part of the page, noting whether each payment has arrived. Before comparing any new amount with a take-home target, check what costs and tax still need to come out. If you've already subtracted a cost to reach that amount, don't take it off again.

Try to leave with a sentence you can act on. If all you've written is “I want more money,” go back and name what you're going to do tomorrow. “I'll finish this application by Thursday” gives you a task you can start and a date to work towards.
