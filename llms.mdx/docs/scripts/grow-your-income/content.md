# 3. Find and grow your income — spoken script (/docs/scripts/grow-your-income)



**Spoken adaptation · First draft.** Based on [Chapter 3](/docs/chapters/grow-your-income). This is developed prose, not a transcript of a recording.

## The script [#the-script]

What could bring money in from where you are today?

If you have no income, need money coming in, and can work, I think you need to widen the search. Apply for work you can do. Don't leave a job out just because it isn't at the company you had in mind.

It can be temporary. You can still work toward something else.

Now, that has to fit your actual life. Being a stay-at-home parent is a responsibility. Studying takes time. Health, care duties, transport, and permission to work can limit your options. So start with what you can do. If you need support, make checking that part of the plan. If essentials aren't covered today, a possible job next month doesn't close that gap today.

But if the only thing stopping you from applying is that the job doesn't look like the one you imagined, I'd ask you to look again.

I had a conversation with someone at Starbucks about choosing how to get to a goal. Think about trying to get to the top of a sand dune in a Ferrari. You've decided on the car. But does it suit where you're trying to go?

For me, I had this idea of being a tech founder. Then I thought about what I wanted from that. I wanted a say in how a product works, how it's designed, and how it's built. That helped me look at the goal differently.

So what do you want from your work?

If your job gives you the money and the time you need, you don't have to start a business. If it doesn't, we can look at what might change. The hours. The pay. The role. Or something you do yourself.

Let's go back to Michelle. She's our fictional example. Her household plan costs eight thousand four hundred dollars a month. Her partner covers two thousand four hundred. That leaves six thousand for her. Her take-home pay is three thousand, so there's a three-thousand-dollar gap.

She wants more time for writing and research. She also wants to be there for school pickup. So we're looking at money and time together.

She could look at a writing job. She could test an editing service. She could work on a book. Those are options to check. None of them is money coming in yet.

For this example, she keeps her job and starts with one small check. Two hours this week. No spending. She makes a sample with text she owns and asks three possible customers about their needs by Friday. On Sunday, she reviews what she learned.

That's the step. She hasn't closed the gap. She's finding out whether this is worth more time.

And when money does come in, count it properly. In the chapter, we invent a batch of four editing jobs at a hundred and fifty dollars each. That's six hundred received. We assume a hundred and twenty in business costs, leaving four hundred and eighty before tax. We count the unpaid admin and customer-finding time too.

That isn't take-home pay. It isn't a promise of more jobs next week. It's a way to look at what one batch actually gave you.

Okay, now do this for yourself. Use workbook pages eight and nine, or plain paper. Write down your starting point and what limits your options. Compare two possible routes if you have two. What do you know, and what do you need to check?

Then pick one action. Give it a date and a limit on the time and money you'll use. Pick a day to look at what happened.

If your first step is a support application, write that. If it's a job application, write that. If it's a conversation about pay, write that.

You don't need to figure out your whole career today. You do need to know what you're going to do next.
