7. Credit scores 101

Understand the record behind a score and choose one useful action.

First draft · All readers. This chapter uses the United States as its worked system. Reports, scores, access, and rules differ by country. The exercise starts by finding the system that applies to you.

What does that number actually tell you?

Okay, so what is a credit score, and why does this number matter when you want to borrow money?

If you're about to borrow for a home, you want to understand what the lender is looking at. At the same time, if you can't pay for food this week, checking the number over and over won't put money in your account. We need to understand what a score can tell us so we know when to use it and what else still needs our attention.

In the US system, the score helps estimate credit behavior, such as whether someone will repay a loan on time. To produce that number, a company takes information from a credit report and applies a formula called a scoring model. A lender may then use the result to help decide whether to lend to you and what terms to offer. CFPB: what a credit score is.

A score doesn't tell me the life you want, the people you care for, or what you've had to deal with. Those are the things we started with, and they still matter as we work through the credit side. The score gives us one piece of information we may need along the way.

The report and the score

First, let's separate the report from the score, because they're easy to mix up. A credit report is a record that can contain your accounts, balances, payment history, and requests to check your credit. Credit reporting companies collect that information from lenders and other sources. In the US, the three nationwide bureaus are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, and their records can differ because a lender doesn't have to report to all three. CFPB: credit reports, Consumer.gov: checking your report.

A score is calculated using that kind of information. FICO, for example, is a scoring provider; it isn't another name for Equifax. So when you're looking at a number, there are two things to find out: which report supplied the information and which scoring model used it.

That helps explain why you might see different scores in different places. The model, the source of its information, and the date can all change the result. Even the score in your banking app may differ from the one a lender uses for a particular product, so there isn't one number that every lender sees. CFPB: why scores vary.

So when someone says, “My score is good,” we need a little more detail. Which score are they looking at, and when was it calculated? What are they hoping to be approved for? Seeing 720 or 750 doesn't mean every lender will approve the application.

Where did scores come from?

One part of the history is the introduction of the US FICO Score, which FICO dates to 1989. Credit reporting existed before then, so that date tells us when this particular scoring system arrived. It doesn't tell us when every country began judging credit. FICO's history of its score.

The useful thing to understand is why a system like this exists: it helps people make lending decisions. You can learn how it works and use that knowledge when you need it, while still working toward the life you chose in Chapter 2.

When it can matter

Borrowing is one reason to pay attention to this information, because it can affect both the application and the price of a loan. But in the US, reports can also be used in some housing, insurance, service, and employment decisions. What someone is allowed to use and whether they need consent vary, so ask what they'll check for the decision you're making. They may check a report without using a credit score. CFPB: uses of credit reports.

Before you apply, ask what the process involves and what you can review first. Then bring the decision back to your own money. If the offer is approved, can you make the payments and still cover the other things you need? You still have to do that calculation even when the lender says yes.

Let's say a lender offers a fictional reader a $450 monthly payment, but after essentials and existing commitments, that person has $200 left each month. They're $250 short before we even allow for any new costs. Whatever score helped them get the offer, they still need to work out how the payment would fit.

That's why the report and your money plan need to sit beside each other when you're considering an important application.

What to check behind the number

Payment history, unpaid balances, account history, and new applications can affect scores, though the weight given to each depends on the model. Rather than guessing how many points one move will add, we can start by checking the information behind the number. CFPB: score factors.

Look through the accounts and see whether you recognize them, whether the balances and payment records look right, and whether closed accounts are marked correctly. If you see an application you don't remember, make a note to check it. Pay attention to the report's dates as you compare it with your statements: a balance from an earlier date may differ from today's balance without being wrong.

One term you'll come across for cards is utilization, meaning how much of the available credit is being used. To calculate a simple card percentage, divide the balance by the limit and multiply by 100. FICO says its scores consider revolving-credit use, but remember that the balance reported to a bureau may differ from what you see in the account today. FICO: amounts owed.

Let's use an invented example to see how the calculation works for one card and then for two cards together:

CardReported balanceReported limitBalance ÷ limit
A$600$2,00030%
B$0$3,0000%
Combined$600$5,00012%

For the combined figure, we added the dollar balances and limits before dividing, which gives us 12%. Averaging 30% and 0% would give a different answer because the two limits aren't the same. This illustration uses two ordinary revolving cards with reported limits; other products can be handled differently.

Now, does that calculation tell us whether this person can afford the $600? We still need their money plan for that. It tells us how much of the available credit is being used. Treating 30% as a spending allowance would mean buying something because the card has room, without checking whether you have the money to pay for it.

The CFPB advises paying on time and keeping balances low relative to limits. It also says you don't need to carry a card balance to build a good score, so there's no need to pay interest just because you think keeping a balance will help the number. CFPB: building credit.

If you're new to credit and can't see a score, check the provider's explanation before assuming something is wrong. A model may not have enough history to produce one yet, which is different from producing a low score. Understanding which situation you're in helps you decide what to do next. CFPB: understanding credit scores.

Does checking hurt your score?

The answer depends on what kind of check you're talking about. In US credit reporting, checking your own report is a soft inquiry, a category that also includes certain reviews and prescreening checks. Those checks don't affect your credit scores. A hard inquiry, which commonly follows a credit application, can affect them. CFPB: credit inquiries.

If you see a button that says “see if you qualify,” read what happens when you press it. The process isn't necessarily the same everywhere, so ask which step involves a hard inquiry before agreeing to it.

Checking your own report lets you see what's there before you decide whether to borrow. You can do that without making a loan application.

Read it, then act on what you find

If you're in the United States, you can start with AnnualCreditReport.com, where the three nationwide bureaus offer free weekly online reports as checked on October 1, 2026. Keep the report and score distinction in mind here too: access to those reports doesn't promise a free score from every provider. Consumer.gov: free reports.

If you live elsewhere, start with your country's official consumer regulator or credit-report access service, then confirm the provider, cost, and terms before sharing personal information. You don't need to buy a monitoring subscription to complete this exercise.

If you find something wrong on a US report, take that specific item to both the bureau showing it and the business that supplied it through their dispute process. Explain what is wrong and what correction you're asking for, with copies of the records that support you. Keep your own copies and the response so you can follow what happens. If you suspect identity theft, use IdentityTheft.gov as well. CFPB: disputing an error.

Let's say our fictional reader sees a balance on an account they already paid off. They check when the report was updated and compare it with the payoff receipt. If it still appears wrong, they have something specific to ask about or dispute, with records to support it. That gives them an action they can take: send the payoff record about this account and check the response. A goal of raising the score by 50 points wouldn't tell them how to deal with the account in front of them.

If the information is accurate and you don't like what you're seeing, you can still use it to decide what to do next. Bring the payment problem into your debt plan, work out what you can pay, and ask for help if it doesn't fit. We can't promise a particular score or approval date from any one action here, but we can choose a next step based on what's in the report.

Your turn

For this exercise, we're going to look at your own records and leave with one next action. Use workbook page 17: My credit check, or draw five boxes on plain paper: my purpose, my report, any score shown, things to check, and my next action. Keep the report documents somewhere private and use account nicknames on the worksheet; you don't need to copy the full account details into it.

  1. Name the purpose. Start with why you're checking: you might be looking for errors, preparing for an application, or simply learning what's on file. Write that purpose along with the country and the official source you'll use, so the check fits the decision you need to make.
  2. Record what you got. Write the bureau, the report date, and the date shown for the item you're reviewing. If there's a score too, record its model, provider, data source, date, and scale where shown. Use “not shown” for anything you can't see. You can still do useful work with the report alone.
  3. Check the records. Put the report beside your own records and compare the account names, balances, limits, payment history, and any unfamiliar inquiries. If something appears wrong, write down the exact item and the evidence that makes you question it. That will help you explain the possible error when you follow up.
  4. Choose one action. Based on what you found, decide whether the next step is to check with the provider, submit a supported dispute, or adjust an existing payment plan. Give that action a date and keep the response, so you can see whether it resolved the issue.
  5. Set a review point. Write what you're waiting for and when you'll follow up. If you're doing this before an application, bring its estimated costs and payments into your money plan too; checking the report is one part of preparing for that decision.

A filled line could read: “Purpose: check records before a future housing application. Item: paid account still shows $400. Evidence: payoff receipt and report update date. Action: ask the provider to check, then use the dispute process if needed. Review: October 15.” The $400 and date are invented; this isn't a forecast of when a correction will happen.
