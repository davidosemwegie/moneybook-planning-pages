6. Credit cards 101

Read the bill, understand the cost, and choose a payment you can fund.

First draft · All readers. Use your own card terms beside this chapter. The official guidance linked here is for the United States. Dollar examples are invented teaching examples, not current offers.

It still has to be paid back

Have you ever paid for something with your phone and barely thought about the money? You just tap your phone and keep going, and when you're using a credit card that way, it's easy to forget that you're borrowing money each time. Then the bill comes.

That's what worries me about credit cards. I call them a double-edged sword because there are useful benefits, but the ease of spending can make it feel like you've been given free money. And I'm including myself here. Debt management is something I'm still working on too, so when I say you have to be careful, I'm talking about something I need to pay attention to as well.

So before we talk about points or upgrades, let's look at what you've spent and what it will cost to pay it back. Once you understand the bill, you can decide how the card fits your money plan.

Which money are you spending?

A debit card normally uses money in your bank account, while a credit card lets you borrow. They may feel much the same when you tap to pay, but the money comes from different places. Check which account the card uses and whether overdraft is switched on, because overdraft can let a debit payment use money beyond what's in the account too. CFPB: debit, prepaid, and credit cards.

With a revolving credit card, the agreement allows you to carry an unpaid balance. The lender gives you a credit limit, which tells you how much borrowing it allows. So if the limit is $5,000, that's the borrowing limit on the card. You haven't suddenly made another $5,000 that you can add to your income. Consumer.gov: getting a credit card.

A charge card starts from a different idea: the bill is due in full each month, although some products add options to carry certain charges. American Express's US terms, for example, separate the amounts you must pay in full from eligible Pay Over Time amounts. Its “no preset spending limit” feature means spending power can change; it doesn't give you unlimited money. Before choosing one, check the terms for that exact product and country, including when payment is due. American Express US payment terms, Pay Over Time.

My concern with a card like that is whether you can keep up with what you have to repay. If a pay-in-full bill would be hard for you to manage, take that seriously before applying. Loans, lines of credit, and overdrafts have their own terms too; Chapter 5 compares those forms of borrowing.

Three numbers on the bill

When you open the bill, you may see several amounts and wonder which one you're supposed to pay. Start by finding these three labels so you know what each amount is telling you:

LabelWhat you're looking at
Statement balanceThe amount owed when that billing period closed.
Current balanceThe posted balance now, including later activity. Pending charges may be separate.
Minimum payment dueThe least required for that bill by its due date. It is not the price of clearing the debt.

You'll also see a closing date, when the bill was calculated, and a payment due date, which tells you when payment is required. Keep those dates separate as you read the amounts. Your account can still show a statement balance after you've paid it, so check whether the payment has posted and how much is still due. Capital One: statement and current balances.

Okay, let's walk through an invented card bill with ordinary purchases only. The person using it began with no carried balance and still qualifies for the purchase grace period, which we'll explain below. To keep the example focused, there are no fees, cash advances, special payment plans, refunds, or earlier payments.

ItemExample
Statement closesApril 30
Statement balance$800
Minimum due$30
Payment dueMay 25
New purchase posted on May 3$100
Current balance after that purchase$900

The $100 purchase happened after April's bill closed, so it belongs to the next billing period. Under these assumptions, paying $800 by May 25 clears the April statement; paying $900 covers that later purchase too. If you pay only the $30 minimum, you still have $870 of the posted balance unpaid before any later interest or activity, and $770 of that is left from the April statement.

You can see why those labels matter now. If you focus on the $30 in the payment box, it's easy to lose sight of the $900 you owe. Making the required payment and clearing what you've spent are different things.

When interest starts

So what happens to the money you haven't paid back? The card's interest terms tell you what borrowing can cost. APR means annual percentage rate, but don't read “annual” as meaning you have a year before the lender charges you. Many US issuers calculate interest using daily balances, and different transactions can have different rates. CFPB: how card interest is calculated.

Some cards have a grace period, which can let you avoid interest on purchases if you meet the pay-in-full terms. It isn't on every card, and if you carry a balance and lose that grace period, new purchases may start earning interest right away. Cash advances generally start accruing interest from the transaction date. That's why we need the terms of your card: read the agreement or ask the issuer what amount you must pay and whether your grace period is active. CFPB: credit card grace periods.

For a separate, simplified example, let's say $1,000 is subject to interest for all 30 days at a fixed 24% APR. We'll use a 365-day divisor and assume there are no transactions, fees, or daily compounding, with interest added at the end. Under those conditions, the interest works out to:

$1,000 × 0.24 ÷ 365 × 30 = about $19.73.

If you then make a $30 payment, the balance becomes about $989.73. You sent out $30, but only $10.27 reduced the original $1,000 because the rest covered interest. This is just one period using the assumptions above, so it doesn't give us a payoff date or reproduce every issuer's method. It does let us see where that payment went.

That's why making the minimum payment on time and avoiding borrowing costs are separate questions. You can satisfy that month's required payment while still paying to carry the rest. If you have money to pay more after covering your other needs, use the debt plan in Chapter 5 to decide where that extra money goes. Consumer.gov: minimum payments.

Interest isn't the only cost to check. An annual fee can still apply in a year when you avoid purchase interest, and cash advances, transfers, late payments, or foreign purchases may have separate charges. Copy the amounts and conditions from your agreement so you can see the costs together. If you're using a promotional rate, include when it ends and what terms follow it. CFPB: credit card terms.

What did the reward cost you?

I like the idea of getting something back from spending I was already going to do. But that last part matters. Was I already going to do it?

I got an Amex Gold card with an offer that required me to spend a certain amount to get the bonus. If you have costs you were already going to pay and can put those on the card, that's one thing. But you have to be careful about what happens next. You start moving purchases onto it, then buying extra things to reach the target, and you're spending, spending, spending. Before you know it, you owe a lot of money. The bonus is still tied to money you have to repay.

Suppose a fictional card offers a 2% cash reward. The $500 purchase you already planned earns $10 if it qualifies. But if you add $100 of things you didn't want just to earn points, that extra spending earns $2. You've now spent $100 to get $2 back, leaving $98 less in your money plan. That's the part you have to account for when the reward is tempting you to spend more.

Or suppose you pay a $100 annual fee and redeem $80 of rewards over the year. Before considering any other costs or benefits, you're $20 behind. To decide what the card gives you overall, add up the benefits you use and subtract the fees and interest you pay. Be realistic about what those benefits are worth to you: a hotel credit shouldn't get full value in your calculation if you'd never book that hotel without it.

These are invented examples, and real rewards have their own rules for eligible spending, deadlines, fees, and redemption. Check those before taking an offer, then come back to the spending itself. Can you pay for it without relying on money that hasn't arrived?

Make the payment a real plan

Now take the money available after essential needs and your other commitments, and use that to choose a payment amount. Write down when you'll pay and which account the money will come from, allowing for the issuer's processing time and cutoff. You don't want the due date to be the first time you try to work all of that out.

Automatic payment can help you follow through, but check whether you've selected the minimum, the statement balance, or a fixed amount. Those settings can send very different amounts out of your account. You still need enough money in the linked account and a check that the payment went through, so keep a reminder even if you've turned automatic payment on. FTC: using credit cards and paying bills.

If you can't make the minimum, contact the issuer promptly, explain what you can afford, and ask what help is available. If they propose an arrangement, you need to understand what happens to interest, fees, future payments, card use, and reporting. Get the terms in writing before relying on them, because making the call by itself doesn't mean a change has been approved. CFPB: act fast if you can't pay.

And if the choice is a card payment or food, don't hide that problem under a rewards calculation. Use the shortfall work in Chapter 4 and the debt review in Chapter 5. We need to plan for the situation you're in.

Your turn

Have your statement beside you for this part. Workbook page 15: My card statement helps you understand the bill, and page 16: My card payment plan takes you through deciding what to pay. If you're using plain paper, use one sheet per card with the same headings. And if you don't have a card, practice with the $800 statement above; there's no reason to apply for anything to complete this exercise.

  1. Read the record. Give the card a nickname and write its issuer and country, leaving full account numbers off the worksheet. Then bring over the statement date, current-balance date, balances, pending activity, minimum, and due date. Keeping those dates beside the amounts will help you tell what the bill covers and what has happened since.
  2. Find the cost. Look through the terms for the purchase rate, any other rates that apply, fees, and any promotion end date, and copy them onto the page. Find out whether you have a purchase grace period and what preserves or restores it, then write that down too. For anything unclear, leave yourself the note “ask issuer.” You'll know what still needs an answer before you rely on it.
  3. Choose a funded payment. Work out what's available for this card after your other commitments, then record that amount alongside the payment you choose, the bank account it will come from, and the date. Check your payday plan as you do this: if it already includes the payment, don't add it again.
  4. See what remains. Estimate what you'll owe after the payment, marking any interest you don't yet know and any pending charges. Use that picture to decide how you'll handle new spending. If you can't meet the required amount, your next step is the contact action discussed above; write what you'll do and when.
  5. Check the result. When the payment should have posted, check whether it did and record the result. Update your debt record with the amount now owed and set the next review date, so the plan follows what happened in the account.

For the fictional $800 statement, a completed plan might say: “I have $900 reserved for this card. I'll pay the $800 statement before May 25, using the issuer's confirmed timing. I'll keep $100 reserved for the May 3 purchase. I'll check that the payment posted.” Notice where the remaining $100 is going: it's already committed to the later purchase, so you can't treat it as spare spending money.
