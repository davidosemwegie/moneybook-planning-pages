Research notes

Decisions made, sources checked, and factual work still needed for the chapters.

Keep the author's experience and point of view visible. Check the claims that go beyond that experience. A chapter placeholder is not finished guidance.

Decisions made

The author answered these in Plan decisions:

  • Dollar amounts: use generic $ amounts in examples. There is no need to choose between USD and CAD for a made-up calculation. Real source amounts keep their context.
  • Debt: money the reader owes. This includes amounts people might call good debt.
  • Income and debt categories: give a compact map, concrete examples, and a way to handle several sources. Do not use an occupation as an income type.
  • Extra topics: log each question and place it within a chapter as a section. It does not need a page of its own.
  • Promise and title: leave them open.

The book plan assigns all current topics to 12 chapters. Chapter 1 is the first full draft.

Boundaries used in the draft

Income source, payment method, and timing answer different questions. Hourly and salary describe pay methods. A streamer may receive several kinds of payment. Chapter 1 sorts the payment and counts it once.

Borrowed money and transfers from the reader's own savings are not new earnings. One-time gifts and sale receipts are tracked separately so they are not assumed to repeat. Gain on a sale stays with that sale record to avoid double counting. A business's sales and its payment to its owner must not both be counted as separate personal income.

The cash worksheet records costs or tax still to cover. Noncash support is noted separately. The source groups are for planning; tax definitions and reporting rules need the relevant country when those subjects are drafted.

Checking the balances

The author asked readers to see their numbers on paper so they have a starting point for a plan. Chapter 1 now includes current money, income for a stated month, and total money owed. A made-up sheet shows the three totals without adding balances and income together. Other assets stay on a separate list with dates and estimates marked.

Checked September 27, 2026: CFPB's overdraft guidance, updated June 4, 2025, explains that transactions may not update balances at once. The worksheet therefore records the displayed balance labels, dates, and pending items. The CFPB glossary supports the distinction between owned funds and credit limits or overdrafts. No US-specific fees or account rights are generalized to every reader.

The sheet is a record of balances, not a calculation of spendable money or net worth. It uses one current balance per account, keeps available figures as notes, and records negative account balances as debts once. An old statement stays marked for checking; incomplete current totals are labelled partial. Income and support have their own subtotal, separate from other cash receipts. The reason for writing and looking at the figures comes from the author's follow-up direction; no psychological outcome is claimed.

Michelle's rebuilt example

All amounts are explicitly fictional generic-dollar assumptions. The chapter retains the source's desired work, family time, and chosen comforts, then settles a customer-service role for the example. It does not assert an actual school, product price, household, or outcome.

  • Monthly rows total $8,400: $5,900 costs/payments plus $2,500 saving.
  • The assumed partner contribution is $2,400, leaving a $6,000 personal target. Michelle's assumed current take-home pay is $3,000, so the gap is $3,000. Equivalently, $8,400 minus $5,400 current household income is $3,000.
  • The $6,000 moving goal less $1,200 assigned savings leaves $4,800. Over 24 months that is $200 per month, already included in $2,500 saving. The remaining $2,300 is for other saving goals, including a cash reserve.
  • The car payment appears inside transport only. Other debt payments exclude it. The example assumes no further up-front purchases and no additional costs or tax still to cover in the take-home figures.
  • The $200 monthly provision for less frequent costs represents $2,400 per year. Actual due dates remain separate.

The raw $6,500-before-tax/$600 target and $20/$200 weekly cleaning ambiguity are not resolved. The draft avoids treating them as settled facts: it chooses an $800 monthly cleaning allowance without asserting frequency and uses a new, fully labeled household calculation. Local costs, taxes, and future prices need new estimates for a real reader. The script uses the same figures and refers listeners to the full table.

Chapter 3 develops the earning routes and adviser story. It replaces the uncertain sales/app figures with a separate, explicitly fictional editing calculation. Existing Chapter 1 print proofs do not include Chapters 2–8 or workbook pages 5–18; their layout remains future work.

Example and exercise checks

Michelle remains fictional. Her Chapter 2 household target is $8,400; the partner contribution is $2,400; her personal target is $6,000. Current take-home pay is $3,000, leaving a $3,000 monthly gap. Her editing test uses two hours, no spending, a Friday action deadline, and a Sunday review. It has no assumed earnings.

The separate fictional paid editing batch has four jobs at $150 = $600 received. Assumed total business costs of $120 leave $480 before tax. Eight delivery hours plus four customer-finding/admin hours = 12 hours; $480 / 12 = $40 before tax per recorded hour. All customers are assumed to have paid within the reviewed period. These are not market prices, employee wages, Michelle's results, or a forecast of repeating income. With tax unknown, the $480 cannot be treated as take-home money toward her gap.

Pages 8–9 mirror the chapter's five steps: starting point, limits, options, action, and review. They keep possible income separate from receipts, include payment dates and remaining costs/tax, and work on plain paper. All four reader paths point next to Chapter 4, while no-income readers retain their immediate income/support action and shortfall needs.

Recording and arithmetic decisions

Note 9's correction at 03:02 puts bill planning before choosing a saving percentage. The draft preserves the mother's advice, habit-building aim, conditional 25–30% suggestion, Christian tithing practice, and admission of shortfalls. It excludes unclear speech near 16:29, assumed universal pay frequency, guessed phone-plan prices, and claims about guaranteed returns from giving. No biblical text is quoted or interpreted.

All examples use fictional generic dollars and are separate from Michelle:

  • Main month: $5,000 take-home − $2,500 required costs − $1,250 saving = $1,250 left to allocate. Saving is 25% of $5,000. Required costs include later known-bill provisions; no additional tax, business costs, or giving are assumed.
  • Small surplus: $2,000 − $1,600 = $400. Saving 25% of $400 = $100, leaving $300. This is 5% of initial take-home, not 25%. The source's changing $500/$300/$400 remainder is not treated as one settled figure.
  • Separate giving case: $5,000 − $500 giving − $2,500 required costs − $1,125 saving = $875. Giving is 10% of take-home; saving is 25% of the $4,500 after giving. This completion is editorial, not a quoted source calculation or prescribed split.
  • Timing: first $2,500 receipt − $2,000 costs = $500 carried. Add the second $2,500; subtract $500 required costs, $1,250 saving, and $1,250 allocated elsewhere = $0 unassigned. Attempting $625 saving in the first period leaves a $125 gap. The illustration assumes the next month's receipt precedes the next month's costs; readers must check their own dates.
  • Shortfall: $1,500 needs/required payments − $1,200 available = $300 uncovered. Saving, giving, optional purchases, expected assistance, and unconfirmed arrangements do not silently close it.

Pages 10–12 record the allocation, date-by-date plan, shortfall consequences and contacts, and payday review. Projected receipts stay distinct from cash available now. Transfers and reservations must not be counted twice. Actual consequences, assistance, due-date changes, fees, tax, and coverage remain dependent on country and provider.
