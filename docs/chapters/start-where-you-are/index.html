1. Start where you are

Put what you have, what comes in, and what you owe on paper so you can make a plan.

First draft. The source notes are at the end.

Before we talk about what to do with your money, let's get a clear picture of where you are. How much do you have right now, what money comes in, and how much do you owe? Those are the questions we're going to work through first.

You could have a job that pays a lot and still struggle to cover your bills. You could have no job and receive money from somewhere else. So when I ask where you are financially, telling me your job title only gives me part of the picture. We need to look at what happens to the money.

I know what it feels like to have debt with no job because I've been there. I lost my job during my honeymoon, and my wife and I moved to New York with debt from our wedding. Our living costs went up, my wife was a full-time student, and I didn't have a job. I spent a lot of time living on credit, which left me with more debt to work through. I'm still figuring my way out of that as I write this.

That's the place I'm speaking from. I have things I've learned that can help, and I also have my own numbers to deal with. We're going to start by looking at yours.

Why I want you to put it on paper

You need to see the numbers before you can make a plan with them. And I mean see them: open the account, look at the balance, and look at how much you owe. Write it on a sheet of paper and put it in front of you.

Talking about money can be uncomfortable, especially when you already know there's something you don't want to look at. But you can't finesse your way around the amount sitting in the account. “I have some savings” leaves us guessing; “I have $1,040” gives us a number we can use. The same goes for debt. Once you've written down what you owe, we can talk about what to do with it.

You don't have to solve the whole thing while you make the list. For now, get used to looking at the number and saying, “This is where I am today.” If you need to take a break, take one and come back. Then we can work through what money you have, which payment is coming up, and what needs to change.

By the end of this chapter, you'll have three lists: what you have, what comes in, and what you owe. You'll put their totals on one page and choose your starting path.

Where does your money come from?

Let's sort income by where it comes from. We'll use four groups.

These are the groups we'll use to build your money plan. Tax forms may sort the same payments in other ways. The map draws on official income definitions.

1. An employer pays you

If you work for someone who pays you, that money goes here. You might be an hourly cashier or a salaried engineer; hourly pay and salary describe how your pay is worked out, but both come from an employer. A sales worker's commission belongs here too when their employer pays it.

2. You sell your own work

This is money from goods or services you sell through work you run yourself. You might cut hair, design websites, sell meals, or run a shop. If you make videos and earn money from ads or sponsors, that can go here too.

The money customers pay is your sales revenue. But running the business costs money, so we need to keep those costs beside the sales before deciding how much the work gives you.

3. What you own pays you

You may receive interest on savings, dividends from shares, or rent from a property. You may also receive royalties when someone pays to use work or rights you own. The payment comes from something you own, which is why it has its own place on the page.

If “dividend” is a new word for you, it's a payment a company makes to its shareholders. You can receive a salary and dividends, but we'll record them in different groups so you can see where each payment came from.

4. You receive support or retirement payments

If you receive a pension, cash benefits, regular family support, or child support, put that money on the page too. Remember, we're trying to see what comes into your life, including money you receive when you don't have a job.

Keep any limits on how you can use the money beside the amount. If someone helps by providing free housing, write that beside the list as well. That help can reduce what you need to pay, even though it doesn't put cash in your account.

What about streamers and people with more than one source?

You don't have to fit your whole working life into one box. Sort each payment by where it comes from, and use as many groups as you need.

If you earn a salary, run a business, and receive dividends, all three belong on your page. We want to see each source, even when more than one applies to you.

You may come across a payment that seems to fit two groups, such as money from a rental business. Choose the description that best fits the payment and count it once. The same care applies if your own business pays you: adding the business's sales to your pay would make it look as though both amounts were separate personal income. If you're unsure where a payment belongs, write ‘to check’ beside it and keep going. We can come back to the group without losing track of the money.

A deposit is not always new income

Suppose you move $500 from savings into your spending account. The $500 is now in a different place, but you haven't earned another $500. Borrowing that amount also puts cash in your account, except now you have $500 to repay.

We still need to see that cash because it can help cover a cost. Keep another line for money from selling things, drawing on savings, loans, and one-time gifts. Beside each amount, mark whether it can happen again and whether you have to pay it back. That way, we can tell what you received from what you expect to keep earning.

An investment sale gives us another reason to look at where a deposit came from. Let's say you bought an investment for $800 and sold it for $1,000. Ignoring fees and taxes, you gained $200, even though the sale put $1,000 in your account. The rest was money you had already put into the investment. These amounts are an example, not a promised return. Source: Investor.gov's capital-gain definition.

Keep the gain beside the sale record so you can see what happened, but don't add it on top of the $1,000 you received. It's already part of that amount. And before you use the sale to plan next month's spending, remember that receiving the money once doesn't mean you'll receive it every month.

What counts as debt?

Debt is money you owe. It might be money owed to a bank, the $200 you borrowed from a friend, or a bill you still need to pay. Even if nobody is charging interest, you still owe the money, so it belongs on the list.

We'll sort those amounts by how they came about.

The groups help us find what you owe; we'll still need to look at the terms before deciding what to do about it. A home loan and money borrowed from a friend both go under borrowed money, for example, even though their costs, risks, and payment dates can be very different. Debt definitions and examples.

For now, we're looking for the amount you still owe today. That includes a mortgage or a card balance you intend to clear on the due date. Include bills you already owe even when they aren't late, and add the due dates if you know them. A purchase you're thinking about making doesn't belong here yet, because you haven't made it.

Follow how the debt came about when choosing its group. A student loan from the government is still borrowed money. If a court order concerns an existing debt, the order doesn't give you a second copy of that debt to count. The same goes for a loan you've used to pay off a bill: record the loan that's left, and take the paid bill out of your total. If you're disputing an amount, keep it on a separate line while you check it so it stays visible without getting mixed into the confirmed amounts.

Is it good debt or bad debt?

You may have a reason for thinking a debt was a good decision, and we can look at that. Calling it good doesn't change what you owe, though, so it still belongs on the page. To decide whether borrowing helped you, we need to know more than the label:

  • What did it pay for?
  • What will it cost?
  • When must you pay it?
  • Can you make those payments?
  • What could you lose if you can't?

We'll work through those questions in Chapter 5. For now, include the amount even if you think borrowing it was a good choice.

Find your starting path

Now we can use what you've learned to find your starting path. First, do you have income or support coming in? An ongoing source counts even if the payments arrive at uneven times, but make a note if it may stop. If you're living from savings with no current income source, use the no-income row and record the savings beside it. You have money to work with, but we're keeping that separate from having more coming in.

Then ask whether you owe money. Those two answers give you one of the four starting points below.

Your first worksheet

If you have the companion workbook, turn to page 1: What I have now. We'll use pages 1–4 for this chapter.

If you don't have the workbook, grab a notebook or some paper and a pen. I'll tell you what to write at each step, so you can do the whole exercise that way. A document you can keep open beside you works too if you can't or don't want to write by hand.

Write what you know, and if you don't know a number or a word yet, that's okay. Put “not sure yet” and keep going. We're getting a picture of where you are right now; you don't need every box filled in before you can move on. We'll work through the details as we go.

Put today's date at the top so you know when you made this picture of your money. Start with your own money, or, if you're making a shared household plan, use that same household for all three lists. Either way, each balance should appear only once.

Step 1: Write down what you have now

Start with workbook page 1: What I have now, or head your own sheet “What I have now.” Give each place you hold money its own line, with room beside it for the balance, the label used by the account, and the date you checked. Leave some space for notes too, including an available balance if the account shows one. We want to be able to tell where each number came from when we look back at the page.

Open each bank account you use, including your everyday account, savings, and any app that holds money for you. Count the cash in your wallet or at home too. Copy the balance you can see rather than rounding it or writing what you think is in there.

If all you have is an old statement, keep its amount and date as a note and mark the account “to check.” Find a current balance before including it in today's confirmed total. We want to know what's there now, not what was there the last time you looked.

Your app may show both a current balance and an available balance. Copy both with their labels, along with any pending payments, deposits, or transfers. Because transactions can take time to appear, check whether a payment is already included before subtracting it yourself. Otherwise, you could count the same payment twice. Source: CFPB balance guidance.

When we add the accounts, we need one balance from each. Use the current balance if the account shows it; otherwise, use the balance you copied and keep its label. The available balance is extra information about that account, not extra money to add to it.

Add the confirmed positive balances to the cash you've counted and put the result beside “Money I have now: $_____.” If there's still an account to check, call this a partial total so you don't mistake it for the full picture. Keep notes about holds and money set aside for bills beside the result, because some of what you have is already needed for something.

As you add the accounts, check that you're counting each amount once. If an app displays your linked bank balance, you're looking at the same money in two places. A transfer between your own accounts also counts once.

Available credit and overdraft limits stay out of this total because they tell you what you could borrow. If an account is below zero, move the amount owed to your debt list. We'll account for it there; subtracting it from this cash total as well would count that debt twice.

You may also have investments, retirement accounts, property, or other assets. Give those a separate part of the sheet called “Other things you own,” where you record the value shown and its date, marking any estimate as an estimate. Keep them separate from money you can use now, and leave out balances you've already included in the cash list. We want to know about them without treating them as another copy of money you've counted.

If you can't find a number, “to check” is enough for now. Leave that note where you can see it until you find the record; writing zero would tell us something different.

Step 2: List what comes in

Next we want to see what came in over a month. Use workbook page 2: What comes in, or write “What comes in” on plain paper. Choose the month you're looking at and put it at the top, then use your pay records and accounts to give each source its own line. Beside the source, write its group and the amount you received. Our examples use generic $ amounts; use one currency when adding your own figures.

Leave room to note the next payment, whether the amount varies, and any costs or tax still to cover. You can come back to those details if you don't know them yet. For employer pay, record what reached you after payroll deductions. For your own work or rental income, subtract the costs you know, then keep a note of costs or tax you still need to cover. Put “not sure yet” beside anything you haven't worked out. That note needs to stay beside the total so you don't plan as though every dollar is free to spend.

Below the income list, give other cash from savings, sales, gifts, or borrowing its own line. Write the amount, whether you must pay it back, and whether it was a one-time receipt. If someone paid a bill for you, note that help separately too. This lets you see what helped you through the month without assuming it will all happen again next month.

Now add the income entries for your chosen month and write “Income and support received during _____: $_____.” Keep the costs and tax still to cover beside the result, with other cash receipts on their own line. Keep this total separate from today's account balance as well. If your pay is already in the account, it's already included in that balance; the monthly income total tells us how much came in during the period.

Step 3: List what you owe

Now turn to workbook page 3: What I owe, or start a sheet headed “What I owe.” For each debt, give yourself a line for who you owe, what it's for, the amount still owed, and the date you checked it. Leave room beside that for any payment details you know.

Go through your card and loan accounts, gather your unpaid bills, and check for taxes or money borrowed from people. As you copy each balance and the date you checked it, make sure you're taking the amount still owed. A monthly payment only tells us what you're paying at a time; it doesn't tell us the size of the whole debt.

Payment dates, interest, fees, and terms are optional for now. If you know the next payment and its date, write them down, and add any interest or fees you know about. If you don't, leave the space blank or write “not sure yet.” I don't want you getting stuck on a term you haven't learned yet when you can already write down who you owe and how much.

We'll go through interest and fees in Chapter 5: Make a plan for debt and Chapter 6: Credit cards. Right now, we're putting what you owe in one place so you can see it.

Keep your business's debts on a separate list, making a note to check any you may be responsible for yourself. If you know who you owe but can't find the balance, include the name and put “not sure yet” beside it. That gives you something to follow up without having to guess the amount.

Add the balances you've confirmed and put the result beside “Money I owe: $_____.” Keep disputed or unknown amounts beside it so you can see what still needs checking. If any balance is missing, call the result a partial total. The missing amount isn't zero; it just isn't part of what you've confirmed yet.

Step 4: Put the whole page in front of you

Bring those three lists to workbook page 4: My money today, or start a fresh sheet called “My money today.” We can now put the totals beside each other and see what they tell us. In this made-up example, all balances were checked on the same day and there are no pending transactions.

The money on hand, the month's income, and the debt are now on one page, but they still tell us different things. Adding all three wouldn't give you a useful total. We want to be able to look from one to the other and understand where you are.

Use page 4 or your own sheet to bring your results together:

  1. Start with today's date and whose money you're listing, so it's clear whether this is your own picture or a shared household picture.
  2. Bring across “Money I have now” and the total from your first list. Bring its notes too: any missing accounts, holds, pending items, and money set aside. Keep other things you own on their separate list so they don't get mixed into this amount.
  3. Add “Income and support received” with the month and its total. Alongside it, include your current sources and any costs, tax, or amounts you still need to check. That lets you see what came in without losing the details that affect how you can use it.
  4. Add “Money I owe” and its total, with unknown or disputed amounts beside it. As you copy all three totals, keep the word “partial” on any that have missing amounts; moving a number to a new page shouldn't make those gaps disappear.
  5. Use the income and debt answers to write your starting path. If you know the next bill or payment, put it beside that path, along with one number or term you want to come back to. You'll have a place to begin when you pick up the page again.

Okay, put the pen down for a moment and look at the page. You can see what you have, what comes in, and what you owe, along with the things you still need to check. That's enough to give us somewhere to start. Keep adding to it as you find the missing amounts; a few boxes left blank don't stop you from taking the next step.

Keep this sheet somewhere you can return to and update when your situation changes. We'll use it to work through spending and payment dates in Chapter 4.
