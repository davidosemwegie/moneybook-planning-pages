# 4. When you get paid (/docs/chapters/when-you-get-paid)



**First draft · Chapter 4.**

Okay, so you've been paid. What happens next?

My mom used to tell me that when I got paid, I should pay myself first. Put something away for myself. I think there's value in that, because you can spend the whole month working, get paid, pay everybody else, and reach the end with nothing saved. Then you do the same thing again the next month. I want us to make room for a saving habit where we can.

But there's a planning step before we decide how much to put away. We need to know what your money has to cover and when those payments are due. Otherwise, I could tell you to save a percentage that sounds great, and you'd be sitting there wondering how you're supposed to pay your rent.

That's the plan we're making here. It works for wages, business income, retirement payments, or support. If nothing is coming in right now, use the money you actually have and the shortfall section below. Don't wait for a future payday to see what needs attention.

## Look at the bills before the payday [#look-at-the-bills-before-the-payday]

Before the next payday, sit down with your statements, bills, and the money picture from Chapter 1. Look at the whole coming month, including the bills that won't leave your account for a few weeks. Then look a little further ahead. If this month's pay has to cover next month's rent, that rent belongs in the plan you're making now.

For each cost, write how much you need, when it's due, and when you need to send the payment for it to arrive on time. Start with the food, housing, utilities, transport, care, insurance, and required debt payments that apply to your life. Use the bill or agreement in front of you rather than assuming the amount is the same as last month, especially with something like a card minimum.

Some costs won't show up on this month's bills, but you still need to get ready for them. If you're putting a little aside each payday for an annual bill, write down what that money is for. Otherwise, you could look at the account, think you have money left over, and spend what you were keeping for that bill.

Then look at the subscriptions, dinners, trips, and other things you choose to spend on. You can care about those things; just be honest about what belongs where. Netflix doesn't become as urgent as rent because you enjoy watching it. You might need a phone or a car for work, but that still leaves a decision about which one you can afford.

This is why I want you to look at the whole period. You don't want to pay for your gym and streaming subscriptions because the money is there today, then discover you can't cover rent because it comes out later. The order the payments leave your account shouldn't make that decision for you. The [CFPB cash-flow tool](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/documents/7174/cfbp_your-money-your-goals_adjust_cash_flow_tool_2018-11_ADA.pdf) gives a way to map the dates; it is a US consumer-education resource.

## Know which money you're planning with [#know-which-money-youre-planning-with]

Before we add anything up, decide which cash you can use for this plan. If you've protected some savings for another purpose, leave that money out. If a payment is on its way but hasn't cleared yet, mark the amount as committed. We're trying to see what you can still make decisions with, so check that the same balance hasn't ended up in two places.

Put future receipts on a separate list with their expected dates, and distinguish a confirmed payment from one you're only hoping for. A customer saying they'll pay helps you plan, but you still need to check what actually arrives before spending it. We'll come back to that in Chapter 8, because spending a payment before it turns up is something I've fallen into myself.

If the money comes from your own work, it may still have business costs and tax to cover. Separate those amounts before deciding what's available for your personal spending. In the US, independent contractors may need to make estimated tax payments, so check the current official guidance for your situation. The saving percentages we'll use below don't tell you how much tax to set aside. [IRS gig-work tax guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/manage-taxes-for-your-gig-work).

Keep using whichever source labels fit: **Employer, Own work, Assets, and Support / retirement.** You can have more than one. Help that doesn't arrive as cash still matters, but keep it separate from money you can spend. And if you're drawing from savings to get through this period, show that withdrawal without counting it as a new ongoing income source.

## Give saving a place in the plan [#give-saving-a-place-in-the-plan]

Once you've seen what the money needs to cover, you can decide what to put away and what you're saving for. What I care about here is getting into the habit of keeping some of what you earn, when your circumstances allow it. Saving doesn't have to wait until you feel like you're making a lot of money.

If you can afford to put away 25% to 30% of your pay, that's an amount I'd consider. But I mean if you can afford it. If that leaves you short for food or rent, the percentage doesn't work for you. You might start much smaller, and there will be periods when you can't put anything away. That's why we looked at the bills first.

You can start with a small amount that fits your circumstances; the CFPB's emergency-fund guide takes that approach too. If you set up an automatic saving transfer, keep an eye on the account it comes from. You don't want the transfer to go through when there isn't enough there and cause an overdraft. [CFPB emergency-saving guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/).

Let's use a fictional monthly plan in generic dollars. You bring home $5,000, and there are no further business costs or taxes to take out. All your required costs and debt payments come to $2,500, including money you need to reserve for later known bills. We'll leave giving out of this first version and work through it separately below.

| Monthly plan                   |  Amount |
| ------------------------------ | ------: |
| Take-home income               |  $5,000 |
| Required costs and payments    | −$2,500 |
| Saving: 25% of the full $5,000 | −$1,250 |
| Left to allocate               |  $1,250 |

Now you can see what the last $1,250 is available for. You might use some for dinners or other things you enjoy, put more toward debt, or keep more aside. Make that choice while you're looking at the whole plan, so the money doesn't disappear through purchases you never thought about together. You don't have to use it all up.

Now suppose the fictional numbers are tighter: you have $2,000 after tax, and required costs take $1,600 of it. With $400 left, you could choose to save 25% of **that $400 remainder**. That puts $100 into savings and leaves $300 for you to allocate. Measured against the original $2,000, you're saving 5%, even though you used 25% when you worked out what to keep from the remainder.

Notice what changed: in the first example, we saved a percentage of the whole take-home amount. In the second, we saved a percentage of what remained after required costs. Saying “I save 25%” doesn't tell us which one you mean. Write the dollar amount beside the percentage so you can see what the decision leaves you with.

If you have costly debt as well as room to save, compare those uses of the money in Chapter 5. The example here doesn't settle the right balance for everyone.

## Where giving fits for me [#where-giving-fits-for-me]

I'm a Christian, and I believe in paying my tithes. That's part of how I handle my money, and it's part of what I'm sharing with you here. I'm not going to spend this section arguing back and forth about that belief. What I can explain is the way I've made room for it in my own planning.

For a long time, I've found it easier to treat that 10% as already set aside and plan with the rest. If I'm planning to tithe, I don't want to finish allocating all my money and then remember that I still have my tithes to pay. I've also tithed on income before tax. The example here uses take-home income, so you can see exactly what we're calculating.

With $5,000 in take-home income, that 10% is $500, leaving $4,500 for the rest of the plan. You've still earned $5,000; you've decided that $500 of it is going toward giving. Thinking about the remainder from the start is what helps me account for it.

Here's one way the rest could work. This is a separate illustrative version, with saving chosen as 25% of the **$4,500 after giving**.

| Monthly plan with giving        |  Amount |
| ------------------------------- | ------: |
| Take-home income                |  $5,000 |
| Giving: 10% of take-home income |   −$500 |
| Required costs and payments     | −$2,500 |
| Saving: 25% of $4,500           | −$1,125 |
| Left to allocate                |    $875 |

Because we're now saving a percentage of the amount after giving, the saving amount changes too. We have to work through the whole plan again; we can't add a giving line and assume the same remainder will still be there.

If giving is part of your life, put it on the page alongside the bills so you can see what the whole plan asks of your money. This example has room for all of those choices. Yours may not, and if there's a gap, we need to acknowledge it and work through the shortfall section below. Choosing a giving or saving percentage won't make the missing money appear.

## A month can work while a week doesn't [#a-month-can-work-while-a-week-doesnt]

Let's go back to the first $5,000 plan, without giving. Suppose it arrives in two payments of $2,500, on the 1st and 15th. There is no opening balance assigned to this plan. Assume $2,000 of the required costs fall before the second payment, and $500 after it.

If you divide the monthly saving amount in half and take $625 out of that first payment, you'll have $1,875 left for $2,000 of bills. You're $125 short before the next payday, even though the monthly plan looked fine. This is why knowing the total isn't enough: we need to know when the money arrives and when it has to leave.

One possible schedule is:

| Period         |                         Money available | What this part covers                                       |                       Carried forward |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------: |
| 1st–14th       |                         $2,500 received | $2,000 required costs; hold the rest                        |                                  $500 |
| 15th–month end | $500 carried + $2,500 received = $3,000 | $500 required costs; $1,250 saving; $1,250 left to allocate | $0 unassigned after those allocations |

When the second payment arrives, the table gives every dollar somewhere to go. That doesn't mean you send it all out on the 15th. Keep each amount until you need it, and look ahead to what comes due before you're paid again. This example assumes the next payment arrives before the next period's costs; if your dates work differently, you'll need to reserve money for those costs now.

Your own calendar needs the actual date of each bill. Start with the opening balance, add a receipt when it comes in, subtract a payment when it goes out, and carry what's left to the next date. You're following the money through the month. If that running amount goes below zero, you've found a gap that the monthly total may have hidden. The [CFPB cash-flow worksheet](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/documents/10038/cfpb_creating-cash-flow-budget_tool_2021-08.pdf) uses this running-balance approach.

On workbook page 11, you can also subtract money when you reserve it for a bill. If you use that option, the running amount tells you what's still available for this plan, which can differ from the balance your bank shows. When the reserved bill is paid, don't subtract it again. You've already accounted for that money.

If the same dates keep clashing, contact the provider and ask whether a different due date or payment arrangement is available. If they offer a change, find out the new amount, any cost, when it starts, and what you still owe in the meantime. Keep planning around the existing date until the change is confirmed. Canceling and restarting a service doesn't generally resolve a bill you've already incurred. [CFPB guidance on adjusting cash flow](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/documents/7174/cfbp_your-money-your-goals_adjust_cash_flow_tool_2018-11_ADA.pdf).

## What if there isn't enough? [#what-if-there-isnt-enough]

Sometimes you do all of this and there still isn't enough to pay every bill. I'm not going to sit here and tell you that you should always be able to make it work, because I've been in that situation myself. And if there isn't room to save the percentage we just talked about, that's okay. We need to deal with the money you have.

When I'm in that position, I make a priority list. I look at what absolutely needs attention and what happens if I can't pay it. Your list might be different from mine, so write the consequences beside the amounts and dates. That gives you a reason for the order you choose, beyond whichever bill happens to arrive first.

Think about your basic needs and what protects your housing, health, and ability to earn. Include essential insurance and court-ordered obligations as you look at what could happen if a payment is missed. The dates and consequences in your own situation will help you decide what comes first. For bills you can't cover, contact the providers to ask what help or arrangements are available. The [CFPB prioritizing-bills tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing-bills_tool.pdf) works through those consequences rather than giving everybody the same fixed order.

Here's a fictional shortfall for one pay period. You have $1,200 available, after any amounts already committed elsewhere. Needs and required payments total $1,500. There are no optional purchases, giving, or new saving in these figures. You're $300 short.

There isn't a saving percentage that makes those numbers fit. Use the shortfall sheet to see which bills are due and what is at risk, then check available support and contact the providers you can't pay in full. Record what they confirm, including any change to the amount or date. Until confirmed help or an agreed change closes it, that $300 gap is still there. Expected income needs to arrive before you can use it.

Keep any missed payment on your debt record, even if you had a more urgent bill to pay first. You'll still need to deal with what remains unpaid. If your housing or utilities are at immediate risk, or you have a court deadline, seek appropriate local help promptly so you can work with the rules and options where you live.

## Choose tools that help you use the plan [#choose-tools-that-help-you-use-the-plan]

Paper, a spreadsheet, or an app can all give you somewhere to keep this plan. Choose something you can use to see the amounts and dates together, because that's the information you need when you're deciding what to pay.

Suppose an app shows you $500, but you need $450 of it for a bill. You still need to be able to see that commitment when deciding what to spend. Check whether the app includes upcoming payments and whether its transactions are up to date. It can help you keep the records together, but you still have to decide what that money will cover.

The account you use needs to work with that plan too. Look at how you can access your money and make payments, what support is available, and what fees you could pay. Read the conditions for avoiding monthly charges instead of assuming the account will always be free. As the CFPB explains, some US accounts require things such as a minimum balance or direct deposit to avoid a fee. [CFPB account-fee guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/why-am-i-being-charged-a-monthly-maintenance-fee-for-my-bank-or-credit-union-account-en-2151/).

Also check who actually holds the money and what protection applies. In the US, some nonbank services place money at banks, where it may qualify for what's called pass-through FDIC deposit insurance. That coverage depends on requirements being met, so the app's appearance alone doesn't tell you whether your money qualifies. Read the disclosures. [FDIC explanation](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/2023-07/my-money-insured-fdic). Other countries have different systems.

## Your turn [#your-turn]

Now let's do this with your money. Use workbook **[10. My next payment plan](/docs/workbook/my-next-payment-plan)**, **[11. My bill calendar](/docs/workbook/my-bill-calendar)**, and **[12. When money falls short](/docs/workbook/when-money-falls-short)**. If you're using plain paper, give three sheets those same headings and work through the steps below.

1. **Choose the period you're planning for.** Put the start and end dates at the top, along with the currency and whose money you're including. Write the cash available at the start, showing what you've left out because it's reserved elsewhere. List future receipts separately with their dates and how certain they are. Account for any business costs or tax still to cover once, so you can see what you're starting with and what still has to arrive.
2. **Give that money somewhere to go.** Bring your required costs, debt payments, and amounts reserved for later bills onto the page. Then include any giving, saving, and other spending you've chosen. If you use a percentage, write what you're taking that percentage of. An unknown amount means the plan is provisional; keep it visible so you know what to check before relying on the remainder.
3. **Follow it through the calendar.** Put each receipt and payment on its date, including automatic payments already set up. Allow enough time for a payment to arrive where it's due. Add money in, subtract money out, and carry the balance to the next date, marking whether incoming money has arrived or is still forecast. Now you can see whether the plan works all the way through the period.
4. **Work through any gap.** If the balance goes below zero or a need remains uncovered, take it to page 12. Write what could happen, whom you'll contact, what changes they've confirmed, and what remains unpaid. Go back through your choices before treating any remainder as money you can spend.
5. **Check the plan when you're paid.** Compare what actually arrived with what you expected, and confirm the bills before making payments or transfers. Make only those the available funds can support, then record what you did. Review the plan before the next payment comes in, or sooner if something changes.

If you move money between two accounts included in your calendar, label it as a transfer. Count the amount leaving one and entering the other once each; your combined balance hasn't changed. You haven't earned new income or paid a second expense. Money you've allocated to savings still belongs to you, but you've already chosen a purpose for it, so keep it out of the unassigned spending amount.
