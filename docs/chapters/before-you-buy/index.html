8. Before you buy

Check what a purchase adds to your life, its full cost, and the money it leaves behind.

First draft · Chapter 8.

Okay, so you've seen something you want to buy. Maybe it's a new pair of headphones, a camera, or a car you've wanted for a while. You like it, you can already picture yourself using it, and now you're trying to figure out whether you can afford it. What happens to the rest of your money if you go ahead?

I love cars. They make me happy, and I'm not going to pretend that every purchase has to earn me money before it's allowed to matter. Your quality of life matters too. But there's a point where getting the thing you want makes the rest of your life harder, and I don't want you to buy something you can't enjoy because you're worried about the payments.

That's what I want us to work through before you buy it.

What would it add to your life?

Start with why you want it. If the answer is that you'd enjoy it, say that. You don't have to turn every want into a business expense to make it sound serious. What matters is being clear about what this purchase adds to your life, so you can decide what you're willing to spend on it.

Then think about what happens if you wait. If waiting means you lose your way to get to work, that needs a different response from continuing to use headphones that still work. A broken fridge and a nicer camera don't need the same answer, even if they cost the same amount.

If the purchase can wait, you've got room to compare a cheaper option, keep using what you have, or save for the one you want. An urgent need asks a different question: how can you meet it with the money and help available to you now? Keep that distinction in mind when we get to my purchase rule. I use it to pause before unplanned spending, not to leave an essential need unmet.

My “afford it twice” rule

For an unplanned purchase over $250, I try to have enough to pay for it twice. Say I see some new headphones and think, those are sick, I really want them. If they cost $350, I want at least $700 available before I buy them. If I don't have that, I try to leave them alone.

I don't get this right every single time. Something goes on sale and suddenly I've got an explanation for why I should buy it now. That's part of why I use the rule: it gives me something to check when I'm already excited about the purchase. It's a personal rule you can consider, with a threshold that fits your circumstances.

This is for something I haven't been saving for. If you've spent months putting money aside for a purchase, you've already made a plan for it. We still need to check that plan and what you'll have left, but I'm not asking you to save for the same thing twice before using the money you set aside.

Before using the rule, look at which money is actually free for the purchase. If your account has $700 but you need that $700 for rent, you don't have $700 for headphones. Bring forward the bills, debt payments, tax still to cover, saving, giving, and other commitments from Chapter 4, counting each amount once. Then look at what's left.

Even if you pass that check, you still need to decide whether this is how you want to use the money. Having twice the price available doesn't tell us everything about your circumstances or the other things you care about.

Let's check the headphones

Let's try a fictional example in generic dollars, with all purchase charges included and no extra running costs. These are example prices.

You have $700 free, so the $350 headphones meet my twice-the-price check: $350 × 2 = $700. Now look at what happens to that money under each choice. Passing the check doesn't mean you have to buy them.

ChoicePay nowFree money left after buying
Buy the $350 headphones$350$350
Buy a suitable $250 option$250$450
Keep the headphones you have$0$700

Buying the $350 pair leaves $1,450 in the account, which can still look like quite a bit of money. But $1,100 of it belongs to the commitments you already listed, so only $350 remains free. Keep that distinction visible after you buy the headphones; otherwise, you could look at the balance again and spend money you still need for a bill.

Now suppose you have only $350 free because you've been saving that amount for these headphones over time. Since this was a planned purchase, we're checking the plan you made. Has the price changed, or has something happened in your life that means you need the money elsewhere? If your other commitments are still protected and you still want to use the saved $350 this way, you can weigh that choice. Just recognize that buying them would leave no unassigned money in this example. Saving first doesn't remove the need to look at what comes afterward.

You might decide the cheaper pair does everything you need, or that you'd rather keep what you have for now. You could also decide the more expensive pair is worth the difference. Write down your reason and the amount you'd have left, so you're deciding with the whole picture in front of you.

Money coming in isn't money in your account

I've fallen into this doing software work on the side, and I've done it more than once. You have a conversation with someone about a job, they tell you what they'll pay, and in your head that money is already yours. You're thinking about what you can buy before the payment has even arrived.

For an illustration, say you're expecting $10,000 from a job and you spend $2,000 on a computer because you've counted on that payment. Then life happens: the money doesn't arrive when you expected, but you've already bought the computer. You're left dealing with an expense you took on because of money you thought you were going to have.

If the purchase can wait, wait for the money to arrive. I know how easy it is to tell yourself it's fine because you're getting paid next week. I've made that argument to myself too, and it's a very easy way to get into trouble. When the payment does arrive, check the business costs, tax, and commitments it still has to cover before deciding what you can spend. This applies to next week's wages as well as a freelance job.

The same question comes up with a credit limit. Even if your card has room for a $1,500 camera within its $2,000 limit, we still need to know how you'll pay for the purchase from your own money. Bring it back to the payment plan, and use Chapter 6 to follow what happens on the card bill.

An emergency may leave you with harder choices. Put those choices on paper using Chapter 4's shortfall steps. Don't call an optional purchase an emergency just because you want it today.

Buy now, pay later still goes in the plan

With buy now, pay later, or BNPL, you get the purchase and spread what you owe across repayments. You're still borrowing the money, even though you're seeing smaller payments at checkout. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes it as a type of instalment loan. The agreement in front of you tells you how that particular plan works, so check it before accepting. CFPB explanation of BNPL.

What worries me is how easy it is to look at one small payment and think, I can manage that. Then you do it with another purchase, and another. They stack up. Each one might look fine when you're looking at it by itself, but you'll have to make those payments from the same money that already covers the rest of your life.

Before you accept, put the full repayment schedule beside the payments you already have. Write what is due today, each later amount and date, any interest or fees, and the total you've agreed to repay. Then check how the provider takes those payments and what happens if you return the item, need a refund, or miss a payment. An agreement you've used before doesn't tell you the terms of the next one.

For example, the CFPB's US guidance notes that a late payment can come with a fee. If an automatic repayment is taken when there isn't enough money in your account, you can also face bank fees. Whether those charges apply depends on the loan and account terms, so include that check when you read them. Other countries have their own rules. CFPB explanation of BNPL fees.

Let's see how those payments sit beside other purchases. For this invented maths example, the $350 headphones have no interest or fees and are paid for in four payments of $87.50, two weeks apart, starting at checkout. These are assumptions for the calculation, not an offer from a provider.

Look at the dates before October 24. The first headphone payment is $87.50, but you also have $60 and $90 due on other plans. Together, those three payments need $237.50, on top of your other bills. After that first payment, you still owe $262.50 for the headphones. The other plans may continue beyond the dates we've shown too; the table only includes the dates assumed for this example.

Your debt record and cash calendar show different parts of this same purchase. On the debt record, start with the full $350 obligation and reduce it as you pay. On the calendar, show each actual payment on its date so you know when the cash leaves. Adding $350 and all four payments of $87.50 together would make it look like $700 of spending, when you've only bought one pair of headphones.

If you're already behind on your credit card payments and you're considering another loan for something optional, let's be serious about what you're doing. You already have payments you can't cover. Adding another one gives you more to deal with, even if the amount looks small. Go back to the shortfall or debt plan first.

If you can comfortably keep up with the payments, you can consider the option after comparing the total cost and reading the terms. The important part is looking at all your commitments together, instead of deciding from the size of this one instalment.

What about a car you really want?

I love cars. I'm not going to pretend I don't care which one I drive. If a nicer car adds something to your life, you can say that.

For me, I'd want room for twice the payment while still being able to save after mandatory expenses. That's the breathing room I'm thinking about when I say I want to be able to afford the car. But even that personal check needs the rest of the costs beside it. Doubling a payment or choosing a saving percentage won't, on its own, tell you whether a particular car fits your life.

Start with the loan if you're financing the car: how much are you borrowing, what is the annual percentage rate, or APR, and how long will you be making payments? Put the payments and total repayment beside those figures. A monthly payment can look manageable while a longer loan means paying more interest overall, which the CFPB's US guidance explains.

Then add what you'll spend to keep the car: insurance, fuel or charging, maintenance, repairs, registration, and any other ownership costs that apply. Those costs still need money after you've made the loan payment. Use actual local quotes and the contract you're considering so you're comparing the options available to you. CFPB guide to comparing auto loans.

Use the same period when you compare the options, and count the deposit and loan payments once. The payments already cover the financed purchase, so don't add its purchase price again on top of them. Keep annual bills and one-time charges labeled as such. You can set aside money each month for an annual bill, but you still need enough ready by the date the bill is due.

Then ask: what happens in a lower-income month, or when a repair comes up? What would you have to stop paying for? Would this car still improve your life if the payments took away something else you care about?

If it fits and it's something you care about, enjoy it. But if the numbers don't work, you have to be serious with yourself before you sign up. I love cars, and even then, I don't see the point of getting the car I want if I can't enjoy driving it because the payments are making everything else difficult.

Your turn

Pick one purchase you're considering and work through it on workbook page 18. My purchase check. You can use plain paper with the same headings. The aim is to leave knowing what you've decided and why, including what you still need to check.

  1. The purchase and the reason. Write what you want, what it would add to your life, and whether you've planned for it or only just started thinking about it. Then describe what would happen if you waited. That will help you separate something you want today from a need you have to deal with now.
  2. Money available today. Start with cash you can use, then subtract money already assigned to other commitments once. Keep expected payments separate until they arrive. If you've saved specifically for this purchase, include that fund in the available total; don't subtract it with savings for other purposes or add it a second time. You want to see the money this decision can use.
  3. The choices and full cost. Put buying this option beside a suitable alternative and, where possible, waiting. For each, include purchase charges, financing costs, and the costs of using it over the same period. Mark any estimates and details you still need to check, so the comparison doesn't look settled before it is.
  4. The payment dates. Place the upfront amount and every new instalment beside your existing commitments. Keep the full obligation on a separate line, as we did with the headphones. If a later payment depends on income that hasn't arrived, write down that risk and what you'd do if the income were late.
  5. The decision. Now choose: buy, save first, choose another option, wait, or get more information. Write your reason, what money would remain after the purchase, and your next action or review date. If you've used my twice-the-price rule, label it as a personal check for an unplanned purchase. You still need the rest of the picture you've just worked through.
