# 5. Make a plan for debt — spoken script (/docs/scripts/make-a-plan-for-debt)



**Draft adaptation of [Chapter 5](/docs/chapters/make-a-plan-for-debt).** This is a speaking script, not a transcript. The chapter holds full product explanations and country-specific sources.

## Script [#script]

What do you do when you owe money and every dollar coming in already has somewhere to go?

I've been there. I lost my job during my honeymoon. My wife and I moved to New York with wedding debt, and I spent a lot of time living on credit. When I recorded these thoughts, I was still trying to work my way out of that hole.

So let's start with what you actually owe. The card. The loan. The unpaid bill. The money your friend lent you. Even the thing someone told you was good debt. If you owe it, put it on the page.

Now add the details. Who gets paid? How much is due? When? What rate and fees apply? Is anything already overdue? Is there a car or home pledged as backup?

A credit limit isn't your income. An overdraft isn't extra pay. If the money came from borrowing, you still need a way to pay it back. Check your agreement rather than guessing from the name on the product.

Before choosing which balance to pay down faster, deal with right now. What needs paying so you can eat, stay housed, and keep working? Are there notices or deadlines you need help with? Put those on the page.

Sometimes there's a shortfall. I know. If there isn't enough, don't write a repayment plan using money you don't have. Find the gap. Check what can change. Contact the people you owe and find out what options they actually offer. Write down anything they agree to.

Okay, let's say the required payments fit and you have something extra. You could aim it at the highest rate. Or aim it at the smallest balance to get that one finished. Keep the other agreed payments in the plan.

Here's a made-up example. Sam brings home $3,000 this month. His non-debt needs and planned costs take $2,400. He keeps $100 for unexpected needs. That leaves $500 for his debts.

His card requires $80. His personal loan requires $220. He agreed to pay his sister $50. That's $350 required, leaving $150 extra.

He puts the extra toward his card. So the card gets $230, the loan gets $220, and his sister gets $50. All together, $500.

In the chapter's simplified example, $70 of interest is added before those payments. His total debt starts at $5,400 and ends at $4,970. He paid $500, but the balance went down by $430. The statement matters. Some of the payment covered interest.

Now if his income drops, this plan changes. We can't just keep the same extra payment because it looked good on paper last month.

And if money is owed to someone you know, talk clearly. Is it a gift or a loan? What did you agree? If you need more time, have that conversation. Don't quietly treat their money as the payment that doesn't count.

Use workbook pages 13 and 14, or two sheets of paper. First, your debt details. Then, your plan. Write the amount you can pay, the dates, and the next action. If the next action is a call or finding income, write that. It still counts as work you need to do.

Then choose a date to look again. What got paid? What was added? What do you owe now?

You don't have to solve every balance today. But you do need to know what happens next.
