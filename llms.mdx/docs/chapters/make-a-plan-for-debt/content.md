# 5. Make a plan for debt (/docs/chapters/make-a-plan-for-debt)



**First draft · Chapter 5.**

What do you do when you owe money and the money coming in already has somewhere to go?

I've been there. I lost my job during my honeymoon, and my wife and I moved to New York with debt from our wedding. She was a full-time student, our costs had gone up, and I didn't have a job. I spent a lot of time living on credit because there were still bills to pay. It put me in a hole, and getting out was something I was still trying to figure out.

So I'm not going to talk to you like looking at a balance fixes everything. I know what it's like to owe money while trying to figure out where the next money is going to come from. Writing it down gives us somewhere to start, because we need to know what we're dealing with before we can decide what to do about it.

In Chapter 1, you wrote what you owe. Now we're going to add the details: what it costs, what is due, and what happens if you can't pay. Then we'll choose a next step you can actually take.

## Put every amount on the page [#put-every-amount-on-the-page]

When I say debt, I mean money you owe, including the loan you took out for a useful reason and the money a friend lent you for rent. You may have a good reason for borrowing it, but you still have to account for paying it back. Calling it “good debt” doesn't remove the payment.

Use the same three groups from Chapter 1:

| Group                                   | What to look for                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Borrowing or credit                     | Loans, cards, lines of credit, money borrowed from people, buy now pay later balances |
| Unpaid bills                            | Amounts already owed for rent, utilities, medical care, or other services             |
| Taxes, fines, and court-ordered amounts | Amounts due to a government or under a court order                                    |

Include a bill you already owe even if it isn't late. Next month's planned spending belongs in your payment plan; a purchase you haven't made yet isn't a debt. And if you look at an amount and think, “I don't owe that,” mark it for checking. We need to keep track of the dispute without treating the amount as settled or making it disappear from the page.

Now get the latest statement or agreement for each balance so we can add the details. The person or company you owe is your **creditor**. Write their name, the balance and the date you checked it, then the amount due and its due date. Look for the rate, fees, and anything already overdue. If you can't find something, write “unknown” and make a note to ask. Leaving it unknown is more useful than planning as though the cost is zero.

Canada's financial consumer agency also includes lenders, unpaid service providers, government, and people in its [debt explanation](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/debt/plan-debt-free.html).

## What did you agree to? [#what-did-you-agree-to]

Let's say two people each owe $5,000. One has five years to repay it, while the other has a payment overdue today. We can't give them the same next step just because the balances match. We need to know what each person agreed to and what is happening with that agreement now.

The money borrowed is called the **principal**, and **interest** is a borrowing cost charged using a rate. There may be fees on top of that. This matters when you make a payment: some of it can go toward interest or fees, so the amount you borrowed may fall by less than the amount you paid. Your statement should help you see where the payment went. [FCAC: debt costs.](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/debt/plan-debt-free.html)

You also need to know whether the borrowing puts something you own at risk. With **secured** borrowing, an asset is pledged as backup, called **collateral**, and the lender may be able to take it if the agreement isn't met. **Unsecured** borrowing has no pledged asset, but leaving it unpaid still has consequences. What can happen depends on the contract and local law. [FCAC: personal loans, Canadian guidance.](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/personal-loans.html)

### A loan, a line of credit, or an overdraft? [#a-loan-a-line-of-credit-or-an-overdraft]

You might be offered a loan, a line of credit, or overdraft borrowing. Here's how those common structures generally work, along with what to look for in the actual offer before deciding what it would cost you.

| Product                  | How it generally works                                                            | What to check                                                             |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Personal instalment loan | Receive a set amount and repay through scheduled payments.                        | Payment, end date, rate, fees, and early-repayment costs.                 |
| Line of credit           | Borrow up to a limit. Repaying can make room to borrow again under the agreement. | Rate changes, fees, required payments, and how to reduce the balance.     |
| Overdraft borrowing      | The bank covers a payment your account can't cover, leaving money owed to it.     | Interest, fees, repayment deadline, and what happens to the next deposit. |

The comparison uses Canadian official guidance for [personal loans](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/personal-loans.html), [lines of credit](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/loans-lines-credit.html), and [overdraft protection](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/banking/overdraft-protection.html). Terms elsewhere can differ.

A line of credit may have a changing rate, and if its required payment covers only interest, making that payment won't reduce the amount you borrowed. Even if the rate is lower than another product's, it's still borrowed money that needs to be repaid. [FCAC: lines of credit.](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/loans-lines-credit.html)

With overdraft protection, the question is where the money comes from when the payment goes through. A transfer from your own savings uses your money, while a transfer from a credit line adds borrowing. Both may involve fees, so check which one you have and what it costs. [FCAC: overdraft protection.](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/banking/overdraft-protection.html)

Cards get their own explanation in Chapter 6, where we'll look at the bill and what the different payment amounts mean.

### Look past “good” and “bad” [#look-past-good-and-bad]

What did the debt help you do? Maybe it covered an emergency, bought something you still use, or paid for study or work that you hope will help later. Write that down, because the purpose is part of the decision. Then put the payment beside your income and the things you need to pay for now. A useful purpose doesn't guarantee the result you wanted, and even if borrowing made sense at the time, we still need to check whether the payment fits today.

If you're thinking about moving current debt into a new loan, compare the full repayment amount, fees, time, and any asset at risk. The balance is still there after you move it, and a smaller monthly payment can mean paying for longer. You need the total cost as well as the monthly amount before deciding. [FCAC: comparing loan options.](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/personal-loans.html)

## Deal with urgent consequences first [#deal-with-urgent-consequences-first]

Sometimes there isn't enough to pay everything. I'm not going to pretend that never happens, because I've been in that situation too. What I do is look at what absolutely needs to be paid and put those things in order. If rent is later in the month, paying a smaller, less important bill today can still leave you without the rent money when you need it. That's why we need to look at the whole situation before sending money out.

Before deciding which debt to pay down faster, look at what you need for food, medicine, housing, essential utilities, and keeping your income coming in. Put insurance and court-ordered payments in that review too. For each one, the question is what could happen if you miss it and how soon that could happen. If your housing is at risk, that needs attention even when another debt has a higher rate. The CFPB's [bill-priority tool](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing-bills_tool.pdf) uses those consequences to help you make the short-term choice.

If there is a gap, use Chapter 4's shortfall plan and contact the creditor or provider through a verified channel to ask what help is available. Before relying on an option, find out what it costs and record what they actually agree to, including dates and interest. Asking for a change doesn't change the agreement by itself. [CFPB: unpaid bills](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing-bills_tool.pdf); [FCAC: discussing loan payments](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/personal-loans.html).

If you're on the **N-D** path, finding income or support is still a priority. An urgent notice may need a response before that income arrives, though, so keep working on the income step while you get help with the payment problem in front of you.

## Choose where extra money goes [#choose-where-extra-money-goes]

Once you've covered current needs and required payments, we can look at whether there's money for extra repayment. Remember the costs you know are coming too. If you send away the money you need for food, you'll still have to figure out how to buy food afterward. We want a payment amount you can manage alongside the rest of your life.

There are two common ways to choose where the extra money goes:

* **Highest rate first:** you keep up the required payments on your debts and put the extra toward the one with the highest rate, so you're targeting the interest cost.
* **Smallest balance first:** you keep up the required payments and put the extra toward clearing the smallest balance. Finishing that one may help you keep going, though this approach can cost more interest.

You may hear these called the avalanche and snowball methods; the CFPB [compares both](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/how-reduce-your-debt/). We're choosing between them after dealing with urgent consequences, and we still need to check for special terms that could affect the decision, such as a promotional rate about to end or a fee for paying early.

Choose a method you understand and write down why it fits your situation. That gives you something to come back to when your income, costs, or debt terms change. The name is less useful than understanding where the money is going and why you've chosen to send it there.

### Sam's first month [#sams-first-month]

Let's work through a month with Sam, a fictional reader. The generic dollar amounts and contract terms below are invented to show the steps, not taken from a lender's offer.

Sam has $3,000 take-home income this month. His payday plan reserves $2,400 for all non-debt needs and planned costs, plus $100 for unexpected needs. There are no overdue bills, collection notices, or urgent legal payments in this example. That leaves **$500 for all debt payments**.

| Debt                 | Opening balance |      Annual rate | Required payment this month |
| -------------------- | --------------: | ---------------: | --------------------------: |
| Credit card          |          $2,000 |              24% |                         $80 |
| Personal loan        |          $3,000 |              12% |                        $220 |
| Loan from his sister |            $400 | 0%, by agreement |                         $50 |
| **Total**            |      **$5,400** |                  |                    **$350** |

The required payments use $350 of the money Sam has set aside, leaving him **$150 extra**: $500 − $350. He chooses the highest-rate approach, so that extra money goes to the card. Its payment becomes $230: the $80 required payment plus $150 extra. He pays $220 toward the loan and the agreed $50 to his sister, bringing the total paid to $500.

To see what those payments do, we'll calculate one month's interest on each lender's opening balance using the annual rate divided by 12. In this example, there are no fees or new purchases, both lenders allow the payments without a penalty, and the payments happen after interest is added. Your products may calculate interest differently, so use their statements when you do this with your own debt.

| Debt          | Opening balance | Interest added |  Payment | Closing balance |
| ------------- | --------------: | -------------: | -------: | --------------: |
| Credit card   |          $2,000 |            $40 |     $230 |          $1,810 |
| Personal loan |          $3,000 |            $30 |     $220 |          $2,810 |
| Sister        |            $400 |             $0 |      $50 |            $350 |
| **Total**     |      **$5,400** |        **$70** | **$500** |      **$4,970** |

So Sam sent out $500, but he doesn't owe $500 less. He owes $430 less because $70 of the payment covered interest. That's the difference we're looking for when we check the closing balance.

What if he'd chosen the smallest-balance approach instead? His sister would get $200 and his card would get $80. Under the same first-month assumptions, he'd still finish with $4,970 of total debt, but more of it would be on the card and less would be owed to his sister. Those different balances could lead to different interest costs later. We'd need to follow more than this one month to compare the full payoff.

Now suppose Sam's income fell to $2,700. With the same $2,400 costs and $100 reserve, he'd have $200 for $350 of required payments: a **$150 shortfall**, with nothing available for extra repayment. At that point, he needs to revisit the whole payment plan and contact the people he owes. Choosing between repayment methods won't cover the missing money.

## A tax bill or a collection notice [#a-tax-bill-or-a-collection-notice]

When a notice arrives, start by checking who sent it, how much it says you owe, and when you need to respond. Use official contact details to verify it, and keep both a copy of the notice and a record of your reply. Those details tell you what needs attention next.

If the bill is for **U.S. federal taxes**, the IRS offers payment plans and other help depending on your circumstances. You still need to file required returns even if you can't pay in full, and a payment plan may still carry interest or penalties. The IRS's current [tax-debt options](https://www.irs.gov/payments/get-help-with-tax-debt) and [payment guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc202) explain the routes available. If you're elsewhere, use your own tax authority's process.

If someone calls to collect a debt, first verify the company before paying or sharing sensitive information. You need to know who the creditor is and how they arrived at the balance, so ask for those details. If you dispute an amount, keep it marked as disputed while you check it. [CFPB: checking a collector, U.S. guidance.](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-tell-if-a-debt-collector-is-legitimate-or-a-scam-en-1699/)

Under the **U.S. federal debt-collection rule**, covered collectors generally provide validation information that includes the debt details and a deadline for a 30-day dispute period. Pay attention to that deadline: a timely written dispute requires the collector to pause collection of the disputed amount until it responds as required. Read the notice alongside the CFPB's [validation guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-information-does-a-debt-collector-have-to-give-me-about-the-debt-en-331/) so you can follow the process that applies. This doesn't cover every creditor, and you shouldn't assume it applies outside the U.S.

Court papers need separate attention, so get local legal help promptly if you're dealing with a court deadline, threatened loss of housing, or an old or disputed claim. This chapter can help you organize the problem, but it can't set the legal order of your debts or decide whether a claim is enforceable.

## When the money is between people you know [#when-the-money-is-between-people-you-know]

If you borrow from a friend and say, “I'll sort you out,” what have you actually agreed to? You may mean when your next pay arrives, while your friend may be expecting the money on a particular date. You need to agree on the amount, the date, and what happens if your pay is late.

Start by agreeing whether the money is a gift or a loan. If it's a loan, write down the amount, any interest, the payment dates, and what you'll do if things change, then make sure each person has a copy. If you're the one lending, think through what would happen to your own bills if the payment came late or didn't come at all. You need that answer before saying yes. [CFPB: family lending and borrowing.](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tools/adult-financial-education/tips-for-managing-family-lending-and-borrowing/)

That's why Sam's sister is still in his plan even though she charges no interest. They agreed on $50, and choosing to put extra money toward the card doesn't change that agreement. If he needs to change what he pays her, they need another conversation.

## Your turn [#your-turn]

Now we're going to do this with your debt. You'll use workbook **[page 13: My debt details](/docs/workbook/my-debt-details)** to understand the amounts, then **[page 14: My debt plan](/docs/workbook/my-debt-plan)** to decide what happens next. Two sheets of paper with the same headings will work just as well.

1. **Check each amount.** Your starting point is page 3, “What I owe.” Add the details that will help you plan: the creditor, balance date, purpose, rate, fees, required payment, due date, and any overdue or disputed amounts. Include collateral, rate changes, and early-payment terms so those conditions stay beside the balance. List anything you still need to find out.
2. **Mark urgent consequences.** For each payment, write what could happen if you miss it. Add any notices and deadlines, then decide whom you need to contact and by when. This is how you'll see what needs attention before choosing where extra money goes.
3. **Find the real payment amount.** Bring over Chapter 4's plan for the same period and show your non-debt needs, any reserve, required debt payments, and money left. Check that you've counted each payment once. If the result is negative, write down the shortfall and what you'll do about it; there isn't extra money to allocate yet.
4. **Choose a next step.** If you have affordable extra money, name the debt it will go toward and why. If you don't, write the next contact or income action instead. Give the action a date and record any agreement that has actually been confirmed, so you know what you can rely on.
5. **Check what changed.** Set a date to review the plan after payments. When you come back to it, record new charges, the payments you actually made, and the closing balances. If the debt grew, work out why before repeating the same plan.

On Sam's page 14, you would see $500 available for debt, with $350 needed for required payments and $150 extra going to the card. At his review, he compares the next statements with the $4,970 closing total from our simplified example. You'll make the same comparison using your own terms and dates.

If you have no debt, your answer can simply be “none checked today.” Keep the product questions for before you take on a new obligation; there's no need to borrow money just to complete an exercise.
