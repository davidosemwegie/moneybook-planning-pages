### Boundaries used in the draft [#boundaries-used-in-the-draft]

Income source, payment method, and timing answer different questions. Hourly and salary describe pay methods. A streamer may receive several kinds of payment. Chapter 1 sorts the payment and counts it once.

Borrowed money and transfers from the reader's own savings are not new earnings. One-time gifts and sale receipts are tracked separately so they are not assumed to repeat. Gain on a sale stays with that sale record to avoid double counting. A business's sales and its payment to its owner must not both be counted as separate personal income.

The cash worksheet records costs or tax still to cover. Noncash support is noted separately. The source groups are for planning; tax definitions and reporting rules need the relevant country when those subjects are drafted.

### Michelle's rebuilt example [#michelles-rebuilt-example]

All amounts are explicitly fictional generic-dollar assumptions. The chapter retains the source's desired work, family time, and chosen comforts, then settles a customer-service role for the example. It does not assert an actual school, product price, household, or outcome.

* Monthly rows total $8,400: $5,900 costs/payments plus $2,500 saving.
* The assumed partner contribution is $2,400, leaving a $6,000 personal target. Michelle's assumed current take-home pay is $3,000, so the gap is $3,000. Equivalently, $8,400 minus $5,400 current household income is $3,000.
* The $6,000 moving goal less $1,200 assigned savings leaves $4,800. Over 24 months that is $200 per month, already included in $2,500 saving. The remaining $2,300 is for other saving goals, including a cash reserve.
* The car payment appears inside transport only. Other debt payments exclude it. The example assumes no further up-front purchases and no additional costs or tax still to cover in the take-home figures.
* The $200 monthly provision for less frequent costs represents $2,400 per year. Actual due dates remain separate.

The raw $6,500-before-tax/$600 target and $20/$200 weekly cleaning ambiguity are not resolved. The draft avoids treating them as settled facts: it chooses an $800 monthly cleaning allowance without asserting frequency and uses a new, fully labeled household calculation. ### Recording and arithmetic decisions [#recording-and-arithmetic-decisions]

Note 9's correction at 03:02 puts bill planning before choosing a saving percentage. The draft preserves the mother's advice, habit-building aim, conditional 25–30% suggestion, Christian tithing practice, and admission of shortfalls. It excludes unclear speech near 16:29, assumed universal pay frequency, guessed phone-plan prices, and claims about guaranteed returns from giving. No biblical text is quoted or interpreted.

All examples use fictional generic dollars and are separate from Michelle:

* Main month: $5,000 take-home − $2,500 required costs − $1,250 saving = $1,250 left to allocate. Saving is 25% of $5,000. Required costs include later known-bill provisions; no additional tax, business costs, or giving are assumed.
* Small surplus: $2,000 − $1,600 = $400. Saving 25% of $400 = $100, leaving $300. This is 5% of initial take-home, not 25%. The source's changing $500/$300/$400 remainder is not treated as one settled figure.
* Separate giving case: $5,000 − $500 giving − $2,500 required costs − $1,125 saving = $875. Giving is 10% of take-home; saving is 25% of the $4,500 after giving. This completion is editorial, not a quoted source calculation or prescribed split.
* Timing: first $2,500 receipt − $2,000 costs = $500 carried. Add the second $2,500; subtract $500 required costs, $1,250 saving, and $1,250 allocated elsewhere = $0 unassigned. Attempting $625 saving in the first period leaves a $125 gap. The illustration assumes the next month's receipt precedes the next month's costs; readers must check their own dates.
* Shortfall: $1,500 needs/required payments − $1,200 available = $300 uncovered. Saving, giving, optional purchases, expected assistance, and unconfirmed arrangements do not silently close it.

Pages 10–12 record the allocation, date-by-date plan, shortfall consequences and contacts, and payday review. Projected receipts stay distinct from cash available now. Transfers and reservations must not be counted twice. Actual consequences, assistance, due-date changes, fees, tax, and coverage remain dependent on country and provider.
